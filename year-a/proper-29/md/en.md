# Toward Proper 29 · Monday · Lectio, Reading Through

**November 16, 2026**
This week's readings: Ezekiel 34:11-16, 20-24 · Psalm 100 · Ephesians 1:15-23 · Matthew 25:31-46

## What Lectio Is

Benedict of Nursia set fixed hours in his Rule for the monks to read Scripture slowly (*Rule of St. Benedict*, ch. 48). They did not race. They let the word come in. Today, read all four passages twice. The first time, watch. The second time, listen for the line that stops you.

## Four Clocks, and a Fifth

These readings keep four clocks. Ezekiel sits by the river Chebar in Babylon, about 585 BC. A runner has just come in with the news: "The city has been struck down" (Ezek 33:21). No one can date Psalm 100. Pilgrims sang it walking up to the temple gates. Jesus sits on the Mount of Olives in the last week of his life. In two days he will be handed over (Matt 26:2). Paul writes to Ephesus with a chain on his wrist (Eph 6:20).

The fifth clock is ours. November 2026. The church year has one Sunday left, and on it we crown Christ King. A week later Advent comes, and with it the Year of Mark. The year ends by lifting its eyes to a throne.

## Start with the Gospel

The Gospel holds the center of the week. Matthew sets this scene at the end of Jesus' last long teaching. Three stories come before it, and each asks: How will you live while the master is away? This one answers: What will he look for when he comes?

The Son of Man takes his throne. The nations gather. He divides them. That is not the surprise. The surprise is that both sides ask the same thing: "Lord, when did we see you?" (25:37, 44). Carry that question all week.

## Echoes

Sheep wander through every reading. In Ezekiel, God goes out to hunt for scattered sheep. In the psalm, we are "the sheep of his pasture." In the Gospel, the King sorts sheep from goats. Paul names no sheep, but he looks at the same scene from above: God "gave him as head over all things to the church" (Eph 1:22). The shepherd is the king. The king is the shepherd.

One verb ties three readings. In the Greek of Ezekiel, God says he will *gather* his sheep (34:13, *synaxō*). In Matthew the nations are *gathered* before the throne (25:32). And the righteous, Jesus says, *gathered* the stranger in (25:35, *synēgagete*), where English Bibles read "welcomed." God gathers his flock. His flock gathers in the homeless.

## Looking Up

Every reading points up to the same Lord. Ezekiel hears "the Lord GOD" say he will search. The psalm says the LORD made us. Paul prays to "the Father of glory," who seated Christ at his right hand. And the King calls the righteous "blessed by my Father." The one on the throne is no distant judge. He went looking first. He set the table before we came.

## Looking Down

Neither side saw the King. One side served and forgot. The other side passed by and never knew. The darkness here is not loud. It is blindness. We look at people all day and do not see them.

## Today's Practice

Give it twenty minutes, before you leave the house or after the children are asleep. Read the four passages aloud, twice. Sit still for five minutes and let the line that stopped you settle. Write it down. Carry it to work, to the stove, to the hospital. Tonight, look back. Where today, and in whom, might I have met him and not known? Write down one name or one picture. That is enough.

## Closing Prayer

Lord, the year runs out,
and you lift our eyes to your throne.
The people there ask you,
When did we see you?
I ask it too.
Open my eyes this week.
On the roads I already walk,
in faces I know too well to see,
let me find you.
Amen.

# Toward Proper 29 · Tuesday · Meditatio, Chewing the Word

**November 17, 2026**
This week's readings: Ezekiel 34:11-16, 20-24 · Psalm 100 · Ephesians 1:15-23 · Matthew 25:31-46
Today: Ezekiel 34:11-16, 20-24

## What Meditatio Is

"On his law he meditates day and night" (Ps 1:2). The Hebrew verb is *hāgāh*. It means to murmur a word over and over. Isaiah uses it for a lion growling over its prey (Isa 31:4). To meditate is to stand over a text and refuse to let it go. Today we stand over Ezekiel 34.

## Before and After

Read around the chapter. In chapter 33 the city falls. In chapter 34 God turns on the shepherds, the kings of Israel. They fed themselves. They ate the fat and wore the wool. "The weak you have not strengthened, the sick you have not healed, the injured you have not bound up, the strayed you have not brought back, the lost you have not sought" (34:4). In chapters 36 and 37 God promises a new heart and breath for dry bones. Chapter 34 stands between the grave and the resurrection.

Mark that list of failures in verse 4. By verse 16, God picks up each verb the kings dropped and does it himself.

## The Verbs

"Behold, I, I myself will search for my sheep" (34:11). The Hebrew says *hinnenî ʾănî*, "here I am, I." Then the verbs come, one after another, like footsteps.

*I will bring them out.* *I will gather them.* *I will bring them into their own land.* *I will feed them on the mountains of Israel, by the ravines, and in all the inhabited places* (34:13). *I will feed them with good pasture* (34:14). *I myself will be the shepherd of my sheep, and I myself will make them lie down* (34:15). The verb there, *arbîṣēm*, shares its root with Psalm 23: "He makes me lie down in green pastures." *I will seek the lost. I will bring back the strayed. I will bind up the injured. I will strengthen the weak* (34:16).

Read them slowly. The sheep do none of it. They are found, led out, carried home, and laid down. That is how Ezekiel saw God and man. Man does not climb up to find God. God comes down to find man.

## Shoulders in the Flock

Ezekiel saw the dark as well. The sheep scattered "on a day of clouds and thick darkness" (34:12). And inside the flock, the strong sheep "push with side and shoulder, and thrust at all the weak with your horns" (34:21). The lost world is not only out there. It lives in the flock, in the quiet thought, This place is mine. So God says, "I myself will judge between the fat sheep and the lean sheep" (34:20).

## Who Is the Shepherd?

God says *I myself*. Then he says, "I will set up over them one shepherd, my servant David" (34:23). David had been dead four hundred years. The Gospel opens the riddle. Jesus sees the crowds "like sheep without a shepherd" and has compassion (Matt 9:36). On Sunday he sits on the throne and divides the flock. Paul says God means to gather all things in Christ, "things in heaven and things on earth" (Eph 1:10). What was scattered, he will gather.

## The Flock Today

Ezekiel spoke to people who had lost their home. We may not live in exile, but we scatter just the same: into long shifts, into hospital corridors, into suppers eaten alone. *I will gather them* is spoken to every sheep who thinks it has been forgotten.

## Today's Practice

Twenty minutes. Read Ezekiel 34:11-16 slowly, three times. Stop at every *I will*. Then choose one verb from verse 16 that fits you now: sought, brought back, bound up, strengthened. Do not hurry to help anyone else. Let the shepherd do it to you first. Today, at home or at work, watch your own shoulders. If you catch yourself pushing, say it plainly: Lord, judge this. Heal this.

## Closing Prayer

Shepherd,
you did not wait for us to come back.
You came out yourself.
You lead out, you gather, you carry home,
you lay the tired sheep down.
I scattered on a dark and cloudy day.
I have pushed the weak aside.
Find me, and judge me.
Bind me up, and heal me.
Lay me down in your pasture
and let me know I have been found.
Amen.

# Toward Proper 29 · Wednesday · Oratio, Praying the Psalm

**November 18, 2026**
This week's readings: Ezekiel 34:11-16, 20-24 · Psalm 100 · Ephesians 1:15-23 · Matthew 25:31-46
Today: Psalm 100

## What Oratio Is

Oratio gives the word back to God. Martin Luther once wrote to his barber, Peter, about how to pray. Take a passage, he said, and braid it into a garland of four strands: instruction, thanksgiving, confession, prayer (*A Simple Way to Pray*, 1535). Today we braid Psalm 100.

## Where the Psalm Stands

Psalms 93 through 99 ring with one cry: "The LORD reigns" (93:1; 96:10; 97:1; 99:1). Psalm 100 closes the set like a doxology. Its title is *mizmôr lətôdāh*, "a psalm for giving thanks." *Tôdāh* is also the name of the thank offering (Lev 7:12). It is a king's psalm. We pray it the week we crown Christ King.

## Seven Commands, Two Reasons

Five verses hold seven commands: shout, serve, come, know, enter, give thanks, bless. The psalm turns twice. Each turn calls, then gives a reason. The first reason sits in verse 3. The second opens verse 5 with *kî*, "for": "For the LORD is good; his steadfast love endures forever, and his faithfulness to all generations."

## All the Earth

The psalm begins, "Make a joyful noise to the LORD, all the earth!" Pilgrims sang it at one gate in one city, yet it calls the whole earth. On Sunday "all the nations" gather before the throne (Matt 25:32). Paul says God put "all things" under Christ's feet (Eph 1:22). This King is no tribal king.

## Two Readings, One Confession

Verse 3 hides a small wonder. The written Hebrew reads *wəlōʾ ʾănaḥnû*, "and not we ourselves." The synagogue reads it aloud as *wəlô ʾănaḥnû*, "and we are his." The two sound alike. Set side by side, they make one confession: We did not make ourselves. We belong to him.

That line walks straight into one of the deepest beliefs of our age: you make yourself. The résumé, the grades, the savings, the eyes of others all whisper it. You are what you built. The whisper is quiet, and it wears people out. The psalm says no. You were made. You are his.

## Echoes

Ezekiel 34 closes with nearly the same words: "You are my sheep, human sheep of my pasture" (Ezek 34:31). On Sunday the King says, "Come, inherit the kingdom" (Matt 25:34). Pasture and kingdom are both received, never earned. Paul calls the Spirit the guarantee "until we acquire possession of it" (Eph 1:14). Those who belong to God are his own possession.

## Through the Gate

"Enter his gates with thanksgiving, and his courts with praise!" (100:4). Worshipers climbed toward the temple step by step. On Sunday the King says, "Come." In the end he opens the gate himself. Every song of thanks we sing walks us toward that door.

## Today's Practice

Twenty minutes, Luther's way. Read Psalm 100 slowly. Ask: What does this line teach me? Turn it to thanks: Lord, you made me. Turn it to confession: Lord, I keep thinking I made myself. Turn it to prayer: Let me live today like someone who is yours. Do it on the train, at the sink, beside a sickbed. Before you finish, read verse 3 softly to someone in your care, or say it for them in your heart.

## Closing Prayer

LORD, you reign.
We did not make ourselves.
We are yours,
the sheep of your pasture.
I keep trying to stand on my own,
on what I have done,
on what others think.
Take that weight from me.
Let me come through your gates with thanks.
You are good.
Your steadfast love endures forever.
Amen.

# Toward Proper 29 · Thursday · Evangelium, Seeing the Gospel

**November 19, 2026**
This week's readings: Ezekiel 34:11-16, 20-24 · Psalm 100 · Ephesians 1:15-23 · Matthew 25:31-46
Today: Matthew 25:31-46

## What Gospel Contemplation Is

Ignatius of Loyola taught his retreatants to begin by "composing the place," seeing where the scene happens (*Spiritual Exercises* 47). Later he taught them to apply the senses: to see, hear, smell, taste, and touch the Gospel (121-126). Today we enter the scene before the throne that way. We use only what the text gives. We add no one it does not name.

## Before and After

Jesus sits on the Mount of Olives (Matt 24:3). The disciples have asked about his coming. This is how he ends his answer. Then Matthew writes, "When Jesus had finished all these sayings" (26:1). Two days later he is handed over. The throne and the cross stand two days apart.

## Compose the Place

See: the Son of Man comes "in his glory, and all the angels with him," and sits on his glorious throne (25:31). All the nations gather before him. He moves them apart, some to his right, some to his left.

Hear: to the right he says, "Come." To the left he says, "Depart." Then hear both crowds answer in one voice: "Lord, when did we see you?"

## Through the King's Senses

Now stop looking at the throne. Look where the King looks. He sees a hungry man, a thirsty woman, a stranger, someone with no coat, someone sick, someone locked up. He says, "I was hungry. I was thirsty." He says *I*.

Then feel it with his senses. He has tasted hunger: after forty days in the wilderness, "he was hungry" (Matt 4:2). He has known thirst: on the cross he said, "I thirst" (John 19:28). He was a stranger, carried into Egypt as a child (Matt 2:13-14). Soldiers stripped him (27:28). "He took our illnesses and bore our diseases" (8:17). They bound him and led him to Pilate (27:2). The steam off a bowl of rice, the cold of a cup of water, the weight of a coat in winter, the smell of a sickroom, iron on the wrist: the King knows each one.

"As you did it to one of the least of these my brothers, you did it to me" (25:40). The Greek word is *elachistōn*, the very smallest. The King hides among the least.

## Looking Up

"Come, you who are blessed by my Father, inherit the kingdom prepared for you from the foundation of the world" (25:34). The kingdom was ready before the world was. It is the Father's blessing, an inheritance, not a wage. The fire, the King says, was "prepared for the devil and his angels" (25:41). It was never built for us. The King's heart has always been to bring people in.

## Echoes

In Ezekiel, God binds the injured and strengthens the weak with his own hands (Ezek 34:16). Here the righteous do the same work. The shepherd's hands reach out through his sheep. Paul says the Christ on the throne has a body on earth: the church (Eph 1:23).

## Discernment

Neither side knew. The righteous served and forgot, because they kept no score. The others passed by and never knew whom they missed. Ask yourself: Where am I most blind? Often it is not far away. It is the people I see every day.

## Today's Practice

Twenty minutes. Read Matthew 25:31-46 once. Close your eyes. For five minutes, see the throne and hear the question. For ten minutes, set before you, one by one, the people you will meet today: a child, an aging parent, a coworker, a family member in a hospital bed. Ask: How does the King see this one? For the last five minutes, choose one thing you were going to do anyway, a meal, a glass of water, an hour sitting close, and do it as to him.

## Closing Prayer

King,
you sit on the throne of glory
and hide among the least.
You knew hunger and thirst.
You were stripped and bound.
Lend me your eyes
to see the ones you see.
Lend me your hands
to do what you are doing.
Do not let me wait for that day to be surprised.
Let me know you today.
Amen.

# Toward Proper 29 · Friday · Instructio, Eyes of the Heart

**November 20, 2026**
This week's readings: Ezekiel 34:11-16, 20-24 · Psalm 100 · Ephesians 1:15-23 · Matthew 25:31-46
Today: Ephesians 1:15-23

## What Instructio Is

Instructio lays out the teaching plainly. John Calvin said Scripture works like spectacles. Hand them to someone with dim eyes, and the blur clears (*Institutes* 1.6.1). Today we read Paul's prayer for eyes.

## The Man in Chains

Paul calls himself "a prisoner for Christ Jesus" (Eph 3:1) and "an ambassador in chains" (6:20). The word is *halysis*, a short chain that bound a prisoner's wrist to the soldier who guarded him. Tradition places Paul in Rome. Acts says he "lived there two whole years at his own expense," with a soldier at his side (Acts 28:16, 30). That would be about AD 60-62, under Nero. Some scholars place the letter in an earlier imprisonment at Ephesus or Caesarea. Wherever it was, the man who wrote it could not leave the room. A soldier stood within arm's reach.

And he wrote that Christ sits "far above all rule and authority and power and dominion" (1:21). That is the weight of the letter. At the other end of Paul's chain stood a soldier of Rome. Paul wrote that Rome, too, lay under Christ's feet.

## The City

Ephesus was the great city of the Roman province of Asia. Its temple of Artemis was counted among the seven wonders of the ancient world. Its theater held more than twenty thousand. When Paul lived there, a silversmith stirred up a riot, and the crowd packed the theater and shouted for two hours, "Great is Artemis of the Ephesians!" (Acts 19:28-34). Magic thrived there. The ancient world even named a set of charms the "Ephesian letters." New believers burned their books of sorcery in public, books worth fifty thousand pieces of silver (Acts 19:19). Paul stayed nearly three years (Acts 20:31).

So when believers in Ephesus heard Paul pray about "the immeasurable greatness of his power" (1:19), they heard: greater than Artemis, greater than the charms, greater than Rome.

## One Letter, Many Cities

The oldest manuscripts leave out the words "in Ephesus" in verse 1 (among them P46, Sinaiticus, and Vaticanus). Many scholars think the letter traveled from church to church across Asia, Ephesus the largest among them. A letter written in chains was read aloud in city after city.

## One Long Sentence

In Greek, verses 15-23 run as a single sentence. Paul hears of their faith and love, and he does "not cease to give thanks" (1:16). He asks God for "the Spirit of wisdom and of revelation," for "the eyes of your hearts enlightened" (1:17-18, *pephōtismenous*), so they will know three things: the hope of his call, the riches of his inheritance, the greatness of his power. That power reached its height when God raised Christ and "seated him at his right hand" (1:20, quoting Ps 110:1) and "put all things under his feet" (1:22, quoting Ps 8:6).

## Echoes

Ezekiel says God will gather the scattered sheep (Ezek 34:13). Paul says God will gather all things in Christ (Eph 1:10). On Sunday the King sits on his throne in Matthew. Paul shows the same throne from heaven's side. Yet Paul does not ask the Ephesians to stare at the throne. He asks for eyes, eyes to see this King in their city, in their homes, in the least among them.

## Witness in the City

Paul's chain told the Ephesians something. You do not have to be free to bear witness. A prisoner can pray for a city. Believers today live in cities too, tied down by work, by family, by the clock. Paul did not hand them a longer list. He prayed for opened eyes.

## Today's Practice

Twenty minutes. Read Ephesians 1:15-23 as Paul's prayer for you. Then set one of your own chains in front of you: the job you cannot put down, the care you cannot leave, the place you cannot get out of. Pray Paul's words: Open the eyes of my heart. Spend one minute praying for your city and your workplace. Today, right where you are tied, watch for one thing God is doing.

## Closing Prayer

Father of glory,
Paul prayed for Ephesus in chains.
I pray to you in mine.
I do not ask first to be set loose.
I ask for eyes.
Let me see the Lord who was crucified and raised,
seated at your right hand, above everything.
In my city, in my house,
let me see your hand at work.
Amen.

# Toward Proper 29 · Saturday · Sabbatum, One Table

**November 21, 2026**
This week's readings: Ezekiel 34:11-16, 20-24 · Psalm 100 · Ephesians 1:15-23 · Matthew 25:31-46
In the synagogue this week: Torah, *Vayetzei*, "And he went out" (Genesis 28:10–32:3) · Haftarah, Hosea 12:13–14:10 (Ashkenazi custom; 12:12–14:9 in English Bibles)

## What Sabbath Reflection Is

Today is the Sabbath, the eleventh of Kislev on the Jewish calendar. In synagogues around the world, people read the same portion of the Torah and the same prophet. We sit at one table with them. First we listen to what they read. Then we say what we read. The table belongs to the Maker of heaven and earth. He chose Israel. Israel does not own him. Neither does the church.

## A Night on the Run

Jacob has stolen his brother's blessing and runs for Haran. Night falls. He "came to a certain place," puts a stone under his head, and sleeps (Gen 28:11). He dreams of a ladder. Angels climb and come down. The LORD stands above it. Jacob wakes and says, "Surely the LORD is in this place, and I did not know it" (28:16). In Hebrew, *wəʾānōkî lōʾ yādāʿtî*: "and I, I did not know."

That is this week's question from the other side. Before the throne the nations ask, "When did we see you?" A runaway in the dark says, He was here, and I did not know.

## What the Synagogue Hears

Jewish tradition holds this night dear. The Talmud teaches that Jacob first set the evening prayer, from the words "he came to a place" (Babylonian Talmud, *Berakhot* 26b). The rabbis noticed that *the place*, *hamāqôm*, became a name for God, "for he is the place of the world" (*Genesis Rabbah* 68:9). From Jacob they learned that God is present even on the night you run.

Then Jacob kept sheep for twenty years. "By day the heat consumed me, and the cold by night, and my sleep fled from my eyes." When wild beasts tore a sheep, he did not bring it to his master. He paid for it himself (Gen 31:39-40). Today's prophet remembers it: "Israel served for a wife, and for a wife he guarded sheep" (Hos 12:12). Here is a shepherd who did the work, the very opposite of Ezekiel's kings who fed only themselves.

The haftarah ends with God's word, "I will heal their apostasy; I will love them freely" (Hos 14:4). It was spoken to Israel. Everyone at both ends of this table needs to hear it.

## What the Church Hears

Jesus told Nathanael, "You will see heaven opened, and the angels of God ascending and descending on the Son of Man" (John 1:51). The church hears in it that Jacob's ladder is the Son of Man. The one on the throne this Sunday is the place where heaven and earth meet.

## One Table

Two mistakes wait at this table. One is contempt: calling the synagogue's reading a mere shadow and refusing to hear how Jacob's night has lived in Jewish evening prayer for many centuries. The other is praise for praise's sake: out of courtesy, staying silent about our faith that the ladder is the Son of Man. Neither one is listening.

Listening means learning from them that God is near on the night we run, and that evening is a time to pray. It also means saying our hope plainly: the One Jacob saw is the King we wait for. The shepherd Ezekiel promised, our Jewish brothers still await. We say he has come and will come again. Here we differ, and we need not pretend we do not.

## Today's Practice

Twenty minutes. Read Genesis 28:10-22. Tonight before sleep, pray an evening prayer the way Jacob did. Think back to one place you passed through today and ask: Was the LORD in this place, and did I know it? Then pray for our Jewish brothers and sisters. Ask the God of Israel, Maker of heaven and earth, to keep them in his faithfulness, and one day to open their eyes to the King who is coming again, the Messiah they have waited for these thousands of years.

## Closing Prayer

God of Israel,
Maker of heaven and earth,
you chose Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob.
You stood above the ladder on the night he ran.
You stood in the places I passed today.
Like Jacob, I say it:
you were here, and I did not know.
Open my eyes.
Remember your ancient people.
Keep them, and open their eyes
to the Shepherd you have set up,
the King who is coming.
Amen.
