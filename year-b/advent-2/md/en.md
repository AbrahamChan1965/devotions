# Toward the Second Sunday of Advent · Monday · Lectio

**November 30, 2026**
Readings: Isaiah 40:1-11 / Psalm 85:1-2, 8-13 / 2 Peter 3:8-15a / Mark 1:1-8
Today: read all four, and find one line that stops you

## What Is Lectio?

In chapter 48 of his Rule, Benedict of Nursia set fixed hours each day for his monks to read Scripture slowly (lectio divina). No hurry. Let the words in first. Today, read all four passages from start to finish, twice, out loud. The first time, watch the pictures. The second time, listen for the line that stops you.

## Where We Are in Time

This week's readings stand at four moments.

Isaiah 40 speaks first, to the exiles. Jerusalem fell in 587 BC and its people were taken to Babylon. By about 540 BC they had lived in a foreign land for nearly fifty years.

Psalm 85 was sung after the return. In 538 BC the Persian king Cyrus let them go home. They got the land back. Life stayed hard.

Mark 1 was written in the late 60s or around AD 70. Christians in Rome had just lived through Nero's persecution, and the temple in Jerusalem was under siege or in flames.

Second Peter was written to the second generation. The apostles were dying one by one, and the Lord had not come back.

The fifth moment is ours: the second week of Advent, a little over three weeks before Christmas.

## Read the Gospel First: A Voice in the Wilderness

The Gospel is the spine of the week. Mark skips the birth of Jesus. His first words are "The beginning of the gospel of Jesus Christ, the Son of God" (Mark 1:1). Then comes a voice in the wilderness: "Prepare the way of the Lord, make his paths straight" (Mark 1:3).

Carry this picture through the week: by the Jordan, a man in camel's hair stands knee-deep in the water. A long line waits on the bank. One by one, people from the city wade in.

## Hear the Echoes

All four passages talk about a road, and about someone coming.

Isaiah says, "In the wilderness prepare the way of the LORD" (Isa 40:3), and "the Lord GOD comes with might" (40:10). The psalm says, "Righteousness will go before him and make his footsteps a way" (Ps 85:13). Mark says John is preparing the Lord's way. Peter says, "The day of the Lord will come like a thief" (2 Pet 3:10), and we are to be "waiting for and hastening" it (3:12).

There is grass, too. Isaiah: "All flesh is grass" (Isa 40:6). Peter: "With the Lord one day is as a thousand years" (2 Pet 3:8). Grass dries up in a day. God's word stands forever.

## Look Up: The God Who Comes Himself

Looking upward, all four point to a God who will not just send a memo. He tells his messengers to speak to Jerusalem's heart (Isa 40:2). He carries lambs in his arms like a shepherd (40:11). He "will speak peace to his people" (Ps 85:8). He "is patient toward you, not wishing that any should perish" (2 Pet 3:9). And Mark says the one coming after John "will baptize you with the Holy Spirit" (Mark 1:8).

## Look Down: None to Comfort Her

Lamentations says it five times: "She has none to comfort her" (Lam 1:2, 9, 16, 17, 21). Why? Because the "lovers" she had chased all turned on her. The comfort this week runs deep because the pain under it runs deep, and so does the sin.

## Today's Practice

Twenty minutes. Read each passage twice, out loud.

Choose one line that stops you. Write it on a card and carry it all day. Your phone's lock screen works too.

Tonight, ask: when did that line come back to find me today?

## Closing Prayer

Lord,
this week I am walking into the wilderness.
No crowds there, only wind and water.
Speak to my heart there.
Let me hear that voice:
Prepare the way of the Lord.
In Jesus' name, amen.

# Toward the Second Sunday of Advent · Tuesday · Meditatio

**December 1, 2026**
Readings: Isaiah 40:1-11 / Psalm 85:1-2, 8-13 / 2 Peter 3:8-15a / Mark 1:1-8
Focus: Isaiah 40:1-11 (with 39:1-8; 40:27-31; Lamentations 1)

## He Speaks to the Heart

Today's key word is He Speaks to the Heart.

Set it back in its verse: "Comfort, comfort my people, says your God. Speak tenderly to Jerusalem" (Isa 40:1-2). "Speak tenderly" in Hebrew is "speak to her heart."

Read it with two other phrases, or it will tilt. The first is **comfort my people**. He says "my" people. She was unfaithful, and he still calls her his own. The second is **her iniquity is pardoned**: "her warfare is ended, her iniquity is pardoned" (40:2).

Without "her iniquity is pardoned," "he speaks to the heart" shrinks into a warm feeling, as if God only says "never mind." Without "he speaks to the heart," "her iniquity is pardoned" is just a court ruling, cold on the page. Without "comfort my people," we forget that this comfort is covenant love, not a stranger's sympathy. Put the three together: to a people who sinned and were punished, God first settles the debt, then bends down and speaks to her heart.

## What Is Meditatio?

Psalm 1 says the blessed person meditates on God's law day and night (Ps 1:2). The Hebrew word, *hagah*, is also used for a lion growling over its prey (Isa 31:4) and a dove moaning (Isa 38:14). The ancients meditated with their lips, murmuring a line again and again, the way a cow chews the cud.

In the twelfth century, the Carthusian monk Guigo II wrote *The Ladder of Monks*. Reading puts food in the mouth, he said; meditation chews it; prayer tastes it; contemplation is the delight of the taste. Today we practice chewing.

## Before the Reading: The Chapter They Cut

The lectionary starts at chapter 40. But just before it sits a heavy story.

King Hezekiah recovered from an illness, and the king of Babylon sent envoys to visit. Hezekiah showed them his treasure house, his armory, "all that was found in his storehouses" (Isa 39:2). Isaiah told him the day would come when everything, even his own sons, would be carried off to Babylon. Hezekiah answered: "The word of the LORD that you have spoken is good." For he thought, "There will be peace and security in my days" (39:8).

The king made sure of a quiet life for himself and signed away his children's future. That is the last line of chapter 39. The next line is "Comfort, comfort my people."

Between them lie a hundred and fifty years. Children grew up by the rivers of Babylon and hung their harps on the willows (Ps 137:1-2).

## Why No One Comforted Her

Lamentations says, "Among all her lovers she has none to comfort her; all her friends have dealt treacherously with her" (Lam 1:2).

The prophets told it as a marriage story. Israel was a wife who left her husband to court Egypt, Assyria, and Babylon (Ezek 23). She thought the great powers would protect her. In the end, the Babylon she chased burned her city.

That is what the Bible calls sin. The Hebrew *chata* means an arrow that misses the mark. What she missed was God's covenant love, his *chesed*. And an arrow that misses hits someone else. The fathers broke faith; the children suffered. They said, "My way is hidden from the LORD, and my right is disregarded by my God" (Isa 40:27).

## Chew on Three Verbs

Say each line slowly, three times.

**"Comfort"** (40:1): *nachamu*, said twice. Lamentations said "none to comfort" five times. Here God answers.

**"Speak"** (40:2): speak to the heart. Hosea uses the same phrase: "I will allure her, and bring her into the wilderness, and speak tenderly to her" (Hos 2:14). It is a husband leading a runaway wife into the desert to court her again.

**"Pardoned"** (40:2): the Hebrew says her iniquity has been paid off. The debt is truly settled. She "has received from the LORD's hand double for all her sins."

## Look Ahead: Today's Lovers

What are our "lovers" today? Savings, a title, a relationship, a reputation. We think they will keep us safe. Sometimes they turn and burn our city, and our children's city too.

Some of us will think of our parents here, and of choices we inherited. Lamentations says that too: "Our fathers sinned, and are no more; and we bear their iniquities" (Lam 5:7). To people like that, God also says, "Comfort my people."

## Today's Practice

Twenty minutes.

1. Read Isaiah 39, then 40:1-11. Feel the drop between the two chapters.
2. Choose one verb (comfort, speak, pardoned). Murmur it over and over. Chew it.
3. Ask: on which "lover" have I staked my safety? Write it down.
4. Be still for one minute. Let God speak one sentence to your heart. Don't rush to fill the silence.

## Closing Prayer

Lord,
I have chased other lovers too.
For my own quiet, I let others pay.
You did not send me a notice.
You bent down and spoke to my heart.
You said, Comfort, comfort my people.
You said, Her iniquity is pardoned.
I am one of your people.
Say it to me again. Amen.

# Toward the Second Sunday of Advent · Wednesday · Oratio

**December 2, 2026**
Readings: Isaiah 40:1-11 / Psalm 85:1-2, 8-13 / 2 Peter 3:8-15a / Mark 1:1-8
Focus: all of Psalm 85 (read back the verses the lectionary cuts, 3-7)

## Righteousness Kisses Peace

Today's key word is Righteousness Kisses Peace: "Steadfast love and faithfulness meet; righteousness and peace kiss each other" (Ps 85:10).

It is a beautiful picture, and an easy one to flatten into "let's all just get along." So read it with two other phrases.

The first is **steadfast love meets faithfulness**, the first half of the same verse. Steadfast love, *chesed*, is love that keeps covenant. Faithfulness, *emet*, is what holds and does not change. This is not a passing warm feeling. It is love that made a promise and keeps it.

The second is **restore us again**: "Restore us again, O God of our salvation" (85:4). The lectionary cuts this part. The people were back in their land, but their hearts had not come home. They knew they could not turn themselves around. They asked God to turn them.

Put the three together. Righteousness and peace can kiss, not because God shrugs at right and wrong, but because he keeps his covenant to the end. Not because we have already improved, but because we ask him to turn us around. And how can righteousness kiss peace at all? Isaiah gives the answer later: "Upon him was the chastisement that brought us peace" (Isa 53:5). The word for peace there is the same as in Psalm 85: *shalom*. The punishment fell on him, and the peace came to us.

## What Is Oratio?

The monastic tradition has a saying: *ora et labora*, pray and work. Benedict's monks stopped work seven times a day to sing the psalms in chapel, then went back to the fields and the kitchen. Prayer was not outside the work. It was the work's heartbeat.

The psalms are the church's oldest prayer book. Today we pray Psalm 85, and carry it into our work.

## Before and After: After the Homecoming

This psalm belongs to "the Sons of Korah." Its first three verses look back: "LORD, you were favorable to your land; you restored the fortunes of Jacob" (85:1).

But in verses 4-7, the ones the lectionary leaves out, the tone changes: "Will you be angry with us forever? ... Will you not revive us again, that your people may rejoice in you?" (85:5-6)

That is what homecoming was really like. Houses unbuilt, harvests thin (Hag 1:6), the shadow of anger still over them. They don't pretend. The psalm says the pain out loud first, and only then hears the promise.

## Listen First, Then Speak

Verse 8 is the hinge: "Let me hear what God the LORD will speak, for he will speak peace to his people."

The first step of prayer is listening. The psalmist finishes his complaint, then stops to listen. You can do the same today: say all of your pain, then pause, and listen.

## Four Who Meet

Verses 10-11 are a painting. Steadfast love and faithfulness meet on the road. Righteousness and peace embrace and kiss. "Faithfulness springs up from the ground, and righteousness looks down from the sky."

In the Middle Ages, Bernard of Clairvaux preached these four as the four daughters of God. Mercy and Peace plead for sinners. Truth and Righteousness insist that sin must be punished. The sisters argue until the Son says, "I will go." He becomes human, bears the punishment, and the four are reconciled in him. (An allegory, so we hold it lightly; but it gets one thing right. This meeting has a cost.)

## Take the Psalm to Work

The last two verses: "The LORD will give what is good, and our land will yield its increase. Righteousness will go before him and make his footsteps a way" (85:12-13).

The prayer ends on the ground, on a road. Take verse 10 to work today. Say it at the sink, at a red light, before a phone call: "Steadfast love and faithfulness meet; righteousness and peace kiss each other."

## Today's Practice

Twenty minutes.

1. Read all of Psalm 85, especially verses 4-7. Name your own pain the way verses 5-6 do.
2. Pause for one minute and say, with verse 8, "Let me hear."
3. Think of one relationship that needs mending. Ask: for this to heal, who has to pay?
4. At work today, stop at least three times and say verse 10.

## Closing Prayer

God of our salvation,
restore us again.
We came home, and our hearts are still far away.
Let me hear what you will speak.
Your steadfast love and faithfulness meet me on the road;
your righteousness and peace kissed at the cross.
The punishment fell on your Son,
and the peace came to us.
Today, let me hear you at work too. Amen.

# Toward the Second Sunday of Advent · Thursday · Evangelium

**December 3, 2026**
Readings: Isaiah 40:1-11 / Psalm 85:1-2, 8-13 / 2 Peter 3:8-15a / Mark 1:1-8
Focus: Mark 1:1-8 (with 1:9-15; 6:14-29)

## Turn and Make the Way

Today's key word is Turn and Make the Way.

"Make the way" can sound like a to-do list: do more, get more ready. So read it with two other phrases.

The first is **make his paths straight**: "Prepare the way of the Lord, make his paths straight" (Mark 1:3). His paths, not my plans. Isaiah spells it out: "Every valley shall be lifted up, and every mountain and hill be made low; the uneven ground shall become level, and the rough places a plain" (Isa 40:4). The despairing are raised. The proud come down. The crooked become honest.

The second is **a voice in the wilderness**: "The voice of one crying in the wilderness" (Mark 1:3). The road is built in the desert, not in the temple courts. People have to hear that voice before they will walk out.

Put the three together. "Turn" is repentance, *metanoia*, a mind that turns right around. People hear the voice in the wilderness, walk out of the city, and turn around in the river. The road that gets straightened is not out there. It is the heart.

## What Is Gospel Contemplation?

In the *Spiritual Exercises*, Ignatius of Loyola teaches us to contemplate the Gospels. First, compose the place: picture it. Then enter with all five senses. Then speak with the Lord. Last, examine your day. Today we walk into Mark 1 this way.

## Compose the Place: The Jordan

Picture yourself on the lower Jordan, not far from Jericho. You are hundreds of meters below sea level. The air is dry and hot. Tan hills all around, no trees. Only a thin green ribbon along the river: reeds, tamarisks, muddy brown water.

The bank is crowded. Some walked downhill all day from Jerusalem, feet grey with dust. Priestly families in fine linen. Tax collectors. Soldiers.

## Enter with the Senses

**See:** a man standing in the shallows. He wears camel's hair and a leather belt (Mark 1:6), like Elijah long ago (2 Kgs 1:8). His skin is burned dark.

**Hear:** his voice is loud, louder than the water: "Repent!" One by one, people step down into the river. Splashing. Someone in the water starts to sob.

**Smell:** river mud, sweat, and the salt drifting up from the Dead Sea.

**Taste:** he eats locusts and wild honey (1:6). Desert food, crunchy and sweet.

**Touch:** you wade in. The water is colder than you expected. The mud is soft underfoot. His hand presses your shoulder, and pushes you under.

## The Strangest Direction

"All the country of Judea and all Jerusalem were going out to him and were being baptized by him in the river Jordan, confessing their sins" (1:5).

The city had the temple, the priests, the altar. Yet people left the city and walked into the desert. As if to say: the real road has to start again outside the walls. It is Israel's old story: forty years in the wilderness, then across the Jordan into the promised land.

## The Voice That Was Handed Over

Read Mark 6:14-29. John tells Herod, "It is not lawful for you to have your brother's wife." He is jailed, and in the end his head is carried in on a platter.

Mark says John was "handed over" (1:14), and later uses the same word for Jesus, "delivered into the hands of men" (9:31). The man who built the road walked it first.

Now read 1:9-11. Jesus steps into the same river. "When he came up out of the water, immediately he saw the heavens being torn open" (1:10). The one who had nothing to repent of stood in line with those who did, in the same river as us.

## Speak with the Lord

At the river's edge, talk with Jesus. You might begin, "Lord, I've been standing on the bank a long time. I haven't dared to go in, because..." Finish the sentence. Then listen.

## Today's Practice: Evening Examen

Ten minutes before bed.

1. Look back over the day. When did I hear a "voice in the wilderness"? A nudge, a sting of conscience?
2. When did I not turn?
3. Tomorrow, which stretch of road needs straightening? A harsh word, an account to correct, an apology never said?
4. Thank the Lord that he stands in this river too.

## Closing Prayer

Lord Jesus,
you stand among us,
in the line of the repentant, in the cold river water.
I hear the voice in the wilderness:
Make his paths straight.
My heart has hills and valleys,
crooked places and rough ground.
Today I will turn around.
Baptize me with your Holy Spirit. Amen.

# Toward the Second Sunday of Advent · Friday · Confirmatio

**December 4, 2026**
Readings: Isaiah 40:1-11 / Psalm 85:1-2, 8-13 / 2 Peter 3:8-15a / Mark 1:1-8
Focus: 2 Peter 3:1-18 (with Romans 3:21-26)

## He Waits for You

Today's key word is He Waits for You: "The Lord is not slow to fulfill his promise as some count slowness, but is patient toward you, not wishing that any should perish, but that all should reach repentance" (2 Pet 3:9). "Patient" is *makrothymeō*, long-tempered: holding anger back a very long time.

"He waits for you" sounds warm. It is also easy to misread. So read it with two other phrases.

The first is **a thousand years as one day**: "With the Lord one day is as a thousand years, and a thousand years as one day" (3:8). The line comes from the prayer of Moses: "A thousand years in your sight are but as yesterday when it is past, or as a watch in the night" (Ps 90:4). It cuts both ways. A thousand years as one day: what feels endless to us is like yesterday to God. He is not late. One day as a thousand years: God takes each of our days seriously. A single turning of the heart today carries a thousand years' weight in his eyes.

The second is **he is not slow**. God has not forgotten. He is not powerless. He does not shrug at sin. His patience has a ground and an end. Paul says God passed over former sins "in his divine forbearance" because he "put forward" Jesus "as a propitiation by his blood," "so that he might be just and the justifier of the one who has faith in Jesus" (Rom 3:25-26). God can be patient because of the cross. Every extra day he waits is held up by his Son's blood.

Put the three together. He waits for you, not like a soft old man who looks the other way, but with a patience that was paid for. Not late, but on his own clock. And not forever: "The day of the Lord will come like a thief" (3:10).

## What Is Confirmatio?

Paul says, "Be imitators of me, as I am of Christ" (1 Cor 11:1). To imitate Christ is to live his life in the Holy Spirit, in fellowship with the Father, the Son, and the Spirit. The road has two battles: one against the desires of the flesh, one against this present age. Today we learn from 2 Peter.

## Written to the Second Generation

When 2 Peter was written, the apostles were dying one by one. People mocked: "Where is the promise of his coming? For ever since the fathers fell asleep, all things are continuing as they were from the beginning of creation" (3:4).

"Fell asleep" means died. Parents waited all their lives and died without seeing it. Their children asked: do we keep waiting?

Peter reminds them of two things. First, the world has not always "continued as it was": God once judged it by water (3:6). Second, God does not see time the way we do.

## How People Who Wait Should Live

"What sort of people ought you to be in lives of holiness and godliness, waiting for and hastening the coming of the day of God" (3:11-12).

Waiting is not sitting idle. It is living in a way that welcomes him. Peter says, "Be diligent to be found by him without spot or blemish, and at peace. And count the patience of our Lord as salvation" (3:14-15).

## Two Battles

**Against the flesh:** the flesh says "later." Apologize later, pray later, change later. But one day is as a thousand years. Today's turning matters. To imitate Christ is to turn today.

**Against this age:** the age says "all things continue as they were." That's just how the world is; it won't change; the strong always win. To imitate Christ is to believe that "according to his promise we are waiting for new heavens and a new earth in which righteousness dwells" (3:13), and to live that righteousness now.

## In the Spirit, with the Triune God

John said the one coming would "baptize you with the Holy Spirit" (Mark 1:8). We do not wait alone. The Father sent the Son. The Son paid the price. The Spirit lives in us and gives us strength while we wait. Peter's last words: "But grow in the grace and knowledge of our Lord and Savior Jesus Christ" (3:18). Waiting is time for growing.

## Today's Practice

Twenty minutes. Read all of 2 Peter 3, then Romans 3:21-26.

Take this week's fourth step: He Waits for You.

Think of one thing you keep putting off with "later." Take one small step today: a phone call, an apology, a prayer.

Tonight, ask: where did I feel God's patience today? And with whom was I impatient?

## Closing Prayer

Long-suffering God,
with you a day is as a thousand years, and a thousand years as a day.
I think you are slow,
and you are waiting for me to come home.
Your patience is not cheap.
It is held up by your Son's blood.
Holy Spirit, help me
stop saying "later"
and turn today.
According to your promise, I wait for new heavens and a new earth. Amen.

# Toward the Second Sunday of Advent · Saturday · Sabbatum

**December 5, 2026**
Readings: Isaiah 40:1-11 / Psalm 85:1-2, 8-13 / 2 Peter 3:8-15a / Mark 1:1-8
In the synagogue this week: Torah portion *Vayeshev*, "And he settled" (Genesis 37:1–40:23); for Shabbat Hanukkah, Numbers 7:1-17; the prophetic reading is replaced by Zechariah 2:10–4:7 (Hebrew 2:14–4:7)

## What Is a Sabbath Meditation?

Today is the Sabbath, the 25th of Kislev on the Jewish calendar, and the first day of Hanukkah. Last night at sundown, Jewish households around the world lit the first candle.

We sit at the same table. First we listen to what they read; then we say what we hear. The table belongs to the Lord who made heaven and earth. The Old Testament in our hands came to us from our Jewish elder brothers and sisters. We are the grafted branches; they are the cultivated olive tree (Rom 11:17-18).

## A Festival Added Later

Hanukkah is not in the five books of Moses. It was added later.

In 167 BC the Syrian king Antiochus IV took Jerusalem. He sacrificed pigs on the temple altar, set up a pagan idol, and banned the Sabbath and circumcision. Many were killed for keeping the covenant. Mattathias and his sons, led by Judah Maccabee, rose up. On the 25th of Kislev, 164 BC, they cleansed the temple, dedicated the altar again, and kept a festival for eight days (1 Maccabees 4:52-59). *Hanukkah* in Hebrew means "dedication."

Later rabbis handed on a story: only one small jar of pure oil was found in the temple, enough for one day, and the lamp burned for eight (Babylonian Talmud, *Shabbat* 21b).

## What It Means to Them

For our Jewish neighbors, Hanukkah remembers three things.

**After defilement, dedication again.** A desecrated temple is not the end. It can be cleansed and given back to God.

**A few kept the covenant.** Most people went along with the Greek tide. A few would rather die than give up the Sabbath.

**The light should grow.** The Talmud records a debate between two teachers. Shammai said: light eight candles the first night, one fewer each night. Hillel said: light one the first night, one more each night. The practice follows Hillel, for this reason: "In matters of holiness we go up, not down." So the menorah grows brighter every night.

They also set the menorah in a window so the street can see it, "to publicize the miracle."

## What the Synagogue Reads Today

**Torah:** Joseph is sold by his brothers and thrown into prison on a false charge. He interprets the cupbearer's dream and asks him, "Only remember me, when it is well with you" (Gen 40:14). The portion ends with this line: "Yet the chief cupbearer did not remember Joseph, but forgot him" (40:23).

**Prophets:** Zechariah sees a golden lampstand with two olive trees beside it, oil flowing into the lamps without stopping. The angel says, "Not by might, nor by power, but by my Spirit, says the LORD of hosts" (Zech 4:6).

Hanukkah takes this reading because it speaks of lamp and oil, and because it reminds everyone that what lit the temple again was not the Maccabees' swords but God's Spirit.

## What We Hear from Our Side of the Table

John's Gospel tells us Jesus kept this feast too: "At that time the Feast of Dedication took place at Jerusalem. It was winter, and Jesus was walking in the temple, in the colonnade of Solomon" (John 10:22-23). The Feast of Dedication is Hanukkah. Winter; a cold colonnade; lamps burning. People gathered around him: "How long will you keep us in suspense? If you are the Christ, tell us plainly." Jesus said, "My sheep hear my voice... I give them eternal life, and they will never perish" (John 10:24-28).

At the feast of the temple's dedication, Jesus stood in the temple. We believe he is himself the temple (John 2:19-21).

We take three things from this festival.

**He has not forgotten.** The cupbearer forgot Joseph for two years. Exiled Zion said, "The LORD has forsaken me; my Lord has forgotten me." God answered, "Can a woman forget her nursing child...? Even these may forget, yet I will not forget you" (Isa 49:14-15). That echoes this week's Isaiah 40: "Comfort, comfort my people."

**What was defiled can be given back.** This week we have read about sin: chasing lovers, selling the next generation for our own quiet. Hanukkah says a defiled temple can be cleansed. John's baptism carries the same hope.

**Not by might.** The Maccabees' descendants became kings, and they became corrupt. What is won by the sword is often lost by the sword. Zechariah's word speaks to both sides of the table: the light is lit by God's Spirit.

## Two Thousand Years of Grace and Grief

We cannot pretend this table has no scars. In history, Christians persecuted Jews in the name of the cross, sometimes on their very festivals. That is the church's sin, and we confess it.

But two thousand years also hold grace. The psalms, Isaiah, the story of Joseph in our hands: they copied, memorized, and guarded them. Tonight, as they light their candles, we can pray for them as the psalm says: "Pray for the peace of Jerusalem" (Ps 122:6).

Neither side will argue the other round. But we can sit together, hear the same Scriptures, and watch the same kind of light on a winter night.

## Today's Practice

Rest. No hurry.

1. Read Genesis 40 and Zechariah 4.
2. Recall a time you felt forgotten. Tell God about it. Then read Isaiah 49:15.
3. At dusk, set a candle in your window. Pray for a Jewish friend, neighbor, or coworker, and ask God to give them peace.

## Closing Prayer

God of Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob,
you did not forget Joseph,
and you did not forget Zion.
The temple that was defiled, you made holy again;
not by might, nor by power,
but by your Spirit.
Remember our Jewish brothers and sisters,
and give them peace.
Lord Jesus, you walked the temple colonnade in winter.
Walk through our winter too. Amen.
