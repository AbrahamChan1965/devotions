# Toward Advent 1 · Monday · Lectio, Reading Through

**November 23, 2026**
Readings: Isaiah 64:1-9 / Psalm 80:1-7, 17-19 / 1 Corinthians 1:3-9 / Mark 13:24-37
Today: read all four, and find the line that stops you

## What Lectio Is

In chapter 48 of his Rule, Benedict of Nursia set fixed hours each day for monks to read Scripture. This slow reading came to be called lectio divina. It does not hurry. It lets the word come in first. Today, read the four passages straight through, twice. The first time, watch the pictures. The second time, listen for the line that stops you.

Read aloud. The ancients always did. When Augustine saw Ambrose of Milan reading in silence, he found it so strange that he wrote it down (*Confessions* VI.3). Your voice slows you down, and your heart slows with it.

## Four Clocks, and a Fifth

This week's readings stand at four moments.

Psalm 80 is the oldest. It names Ephraim, Benjamin, and Manasseh (Ps 80:2). It is a prayer of the northern kingdom, likely written around 722 BC, when Assyria destroyed it.

Isaiah 64 is a lament sung by exiles home from Babylon, with the temple still a heap of burnt stone (Isa 64:11). Somewhere between 538 and 515 BC.

Mark 13 is Tuesday of Holy Week. Jesus sits on the Mount of Olives, facing the temple (Mark 13:3). In two days he will be arrested.

First Corinthians is Paul's letter from Ephesus to Corinth, around AD 53 to 55.

The fifth clock is ours. Today is Monday of the church's first week. Last Sunday we ended a year before the throne. This Sunday we begin a year on a night watch.

## Start with the Gospel: The Doorkeeper

The Gospel is the spine of the week. Mark 13 is Jesus' last long teaching. When it ends, the passion begins (Mark 14:1).

At the end he tells a very short story. A man goes on a journey. He puts his servants in charge, "each with his work, and commands the doorkeeper to stay awake" (Mark 13:34). Then three times: "Stay awake" (Mark 13:33, 35, 37).

Carry this picture through the week: a man at a door, late at night, not knowing what hour his master will come.

## Echoes

All four readings stretch a line between heaven and earth.

Isaiah begs God to "rend the heavens and come down" (Isa 64:1). The psalm begs the one enthroned on the cherubim to "shine forth" (Ps 80:1). Mark says the Son of Man will come "in clouds" (Mark 13:26). Paul says we "wait for the revealing of our Lord Jesus Christ" (1 Cor 1:7). "Revealing" is the Greek *apokalypsis*, lifting a lid. All four ask the same thing: when will the lid between heaven and earth come off?

There is also a leaf. Isaiah says, "We all fade like a leaf" (Isa 64:6). Jesus says, "As soon as its branch becomes tender and puts out its leaves, you know that summer is near" (Mark 13:28). One dry leaf, one new leaf. Two ends of one season.

## Looking Up: The God Who Tears Heaven Open

Looking up, all four readings point to a God who will not stay in heaven. Isaiah calls him "our Father" and "our potter" (Isa 64:8). The psalm calls him "Shepherd of Israel" (Ps 80:1). Paul says, "God is faithful" (1 Cor 1:9). And Mark's first chapter answers Isaiah's prayer: at Jesus' baptism "he saw the heavens being torn open and the Spirit descending on him like a dove" (Mark 1:10).

## Looking Down: Wide-Awake Sleep

One darkness this week is hard to see. Isaiah's people were not innocent sufferers. They stood on ground scorched by their fathers' broken covenant (2 Kgs 24:3-4). But worse than the sin itself was this: "There is no one who calls upon your name, who rouses himself to take hold of you" (Isa 64:7). They had gone numb.

Our numbness is often wide awake. The screen glows past midnight. News pours in like a flood. We are exhausted, and we are waiting for no one. It is a sleep with open eyes.

## Today's Practice

Give yourself twenty minutes. Before you leave in the morning, or after the children are asleep, read the four passages aloud, twice. Then sit quiet for five minutes and let the line that stopped you settle. Write it on paper or in your phone. Carry it to work, to the stove, to the hospital.

Before bed, take the first step of the week: Tear the Heavens, Come Down. Tell God one thing you cannot put into words, or one prayer that has gone unanswered a long time. Do not polish it.

## Closing Prayer

Lord, the new year begins at night.
I read your word,
and I see people on scorched ground begging you to tear heaven open,
and a man at a door, waiting for his master.
I have a prayer I cannot say well.
Tear the heavens and come down,
into my house, my night, my heart.
Keep me awake this week,
not awake and scrolling,
but awake and waiting for you. Amen.

# Toward Advent 1 · Tuesday · Meditatio, Chewing the Word

**November 24, 2026**
Readings: Isaiah 64:1-9 / Psalm 80:1-7, 17-19 / 1 Corinthians 1:3-9 / Mark 13:24-37
Today's focus: Isaiah 64:1-9 (with 63:7–64:12)

## What Meditatio Is

"His delight is in the law of the LORD, and on his law he meditates day and night" (Ps 1:2). The Hebrew *hāgāh* means to murmur a word over and over. Isaiah uses it for a lion growling over its prey (Isa 31:4). To meditate is not to think a great deal. It is to stand guard over one line and refuse to let it go.

Medieval monks called this *ruminatio*, chewing the cud. In the twelfth century, the Carthusian Guigo II wrote in *The Ladder of Monks*: reading puts food in the mouth, meditation chews it, prayer tastes it, and contemplation is the joy the taste gives. Today we chew Isaiah 64.

## Before and After: The Whole Lament

The reading gives us 64:1-9. The lament really runs from 63:7 to 64:12. Read it whole.

It opens by remembering grace. God led them out of Egypt; "in all their affliction he was afflicted" (Isa 63:9). Then it turns to their sin: "They rebelled and grieved his Holy Spirit; therefore he turned to be their enemy" (63:10). Then the questions come, one after another: Where is the God who split the sea? (63:11-13)

The last line is left out of today's reading: "Will you restrain yourself at these things, O LORD? Will you keep silent, and afflict us so terribly?" (64:12) The lament ends on a question with no answer.

## This Ground Is Judgment

Do not read this lament lightly.

The ground they stand on is not the site of a natural disaster. It is the site of judgment. The kings led the way to idols. King Manasseh burned his own son as an offering and "filled Jerusalem with innocent blood" (2 Kgs 21:6, 16; 24:4). Much of it was done in God's name. Jeremiah stood at the temple gate and said: you steal, murder, and commit adultery, then walk into this house and say, "This is the temple of the LORD," and think you are safe (Jer 7:4-11).

The leaders went wrong. The people followed. Ruin fell on them all. Their children said in the rubble: "Our fathers sinned, and are no more; and we bear their iniquities" (Lam 5:7).

So 64:5 is heavy: "Behold, you were angry, and we sinned; in our sins we have been a long time, and shall we be saved?" This is not the protest of the innocent. It is the cry of sinners.

## The Verbs

To chew Isaiah, take hold of the verbs.

*Rend.* Hebrew *qāraʿ*, the word for tearing a garment (in the Hebrew Bible it falls at 63:19b). In deep grief, people took hold of the collar and ripped it. They say: tear heaven like that.

*Come down.* Hebrew *yārad*, to descend. This is how God came down on Sinai (Exod 19:18, 20).

*Melt.* In 64:7, "you have made us melt in the hand of our iniquities." Like wax near a flame. They feel their own sin melting them down.

*Potter.* In 64:8 the word is *yôṣēr*, the same word for God "forming" man from dust in Genesis 2:7.

Put the four together. God tears heaven and comes down. We melt in our sin. Yet the potter's hands can form the melted clay again.

## The Polluted Garment

"All our righteous deeds are like a polluted garment" (64:6). The Hebrew means a menstrual cloth. The image is dirty and private, and it is meant to be.

The good we are proud of, even what we did in God's name, is stained before him. The line is not meant to make us hate ourselves. It is meant to stop us bargaining with God using "my righteousness."

## The Scorched Ground Today

There is scorched ground today. A family where the elders' wrongs fall on the children. A church where a leader's abuse scatters the flock. A nation that does in God's name what is not God's heart.

Isaiah teaches us, in such places, not to ask first, "Why us?" First confess: "We all fade like a leaf." Then hold on to the one name we have left: "But now, O LORD, you are our Father."

## Today's Practice

Twenty minutes. Read Isaiah 63:7–64:12 as one poem. Then take 64:8 and murmur it over and over: "We are the clay, and you are our potter; we are all the work of your hand." Keep going until it sinks in.

Take the second step of the week: We Are the Clay. If a cup or bowl is near, hold it. Run your thumb along the rim. It was once a lump of mud. Then put one thing you cannot hold back into the potter's hands.

## Closing Prayer

But now, O LORD, you are our Father.
Our fathers went wrong, and so have we.
We have done in your name what was not your heart.
We fade like leaves.
We melt in our own sin.
We have no righteousness to show you,
only one name: clay.
Potter, do not let go.
Wet what is broken,
and set it back on your wheel. Amen.

# Toward Advent 1 · Wednesday · Oratio, Praying

**November 25, 2026**
Readings: Isaiah 64:1-9 / Psalm 80:1-7, 17-19 / 1 Corinthians 1:3-9 / Mark 13:24-37
Today's focus: Psalm 80 (with the omitted verses 8-16)

## What Oratio Is

Benedictines have a saying: *ora et labora*, pray and work. The phrase is not in the Rule itself, but it names Benedict's mind well. Seven times a day the monks laid down their tools and went to chapel to sing the psalms. Then they went back to the field, the kitchen, the scriptorium. When the bell rang, Benedict said, "let nothing be put before the work of God" (*Rule* 43). He also told the cellarer to treat the pots and pans "as if they were the sacred vessels of the altar" (*Rule* 31).

So prayer does not leave work behind. It carries work into prayer, and prayer back into work. The psalms are the book for this kind of prayer. Today we pray Psalm 80.

## Before and After

Psalm 80 is "a Psalm of Asaph." Asaph was a temple singer in David's day (1 Chr 16:5). The psalm names Ephraim, Benjamin, and Manasseh (80:2), all northern tribes. Many scholars think it was written when Assyria broke the north.

Psalm 79 weeps for Jerusalem. Psalm 80 weeps for Samaria. Psalm 81 calls the people to keep the feast. The tears of south and north sit side by side in one hymnbook.

## A Refrain That Grows

The psalm has a refrain, sung three times. Each time, the name of God grows:

"Restore us, O God; let your face shine, that we may be saved!" (80:3)
"Restore us, O God of hosts…" (80:7)
"Restore us, O LORD God of hosts…" (80:19)

The longer they pray, the fuller their cry. Waiting does not drive them away from God. It teaches them to call him by his whole name.

"Let your face shine" comes from the priestly blessing: "The LORD make his face to shine upon you" (Num 6:25). They are not asking for a thing. They are asking God to turn his face toward them.

## The Vine That Was Cut

The reading leaves out verses 8 to 16. Read them back in.

God brought a vine out of Egypt and planted it in the land. It took deep root; its shade covered the mountains (80:8-10). Then its walls were broken down. Everyone passing by plucked its fruit. "The boar from the forest ravages it" (80:12-13). "They have burned it with fire; they have cut it down" (80:16).

The vine is Israel. Isaiah 5 sings of the same vine: God planted a vineyard and looked for grapes, and it yielded wild grapes (Isa 5:1-7). This year in Mark we will hear Jesus tell a parable about a vineyard in chapter 12. Right after it comes this week's chapter 13.

## The Man at Your Right Hand

In verse 17 the psalm prays: "But let your hand be on the man of your right hand, the son of man whom you have made strong for yourself!" The Hebrew is *ben-ʾādām*, "son of man."

The psalmist asks God to strengthen one man, so the vineyard may live again. The church hears Mark 13:26: "They will see the Son of Man coming in clouds."

## Take the Refrain to Work

This Wednesday, carry the refrain of Psalm 80 into your work.

When the bell rang, the monks in the field laid down their hoes and went to sing. We have no bell, but we have other signals: the computer starting up, the red light at the corner, the kettle coming to a boil, helping a parent out of bed. Each time, take one breath and say: "O God, let your face shine."

This is pray and work: prayer passing through work like thread through cloth.

## Today's Practice

Twenty minutes. Read Psalm 80 aloud, all of it, verses 8 to 16 included. Then pray the refrain three times: first for yourself, then for your family, then for the church. Each time, call God by a fuller name.

During the day, choose three things you will surely do: starting the computer, washing dishes, getting in the car. Let them be your bells. Each time, say: "Let your face shine, that we may be saved."

## Closing Prayer

Shepherd of Israel,
you who are enthroned upon the cherubim, shine forth.
The walls of your vine are broken down.
My home and my church have places that have been trampled too.
O God, O God of hosts, O LORD God of hosts,
restore us.
Today, at my work,
I will call on your name again and again.
Let your face shine
on my desk and on my hands,
and we shall be saved. Amen.

# Toward Advent 1 · Thursday · Evangelium, Gospel Contemplation

**November 26, 2026**
Readings: Isaiah 64:1-9 / Psalm 80:1-7, 17-19 / 1 Corinthians 1:3-9 / Mark 13:24-37
Today's focus: Mark 13:24-37 (with 14:32-42)

## What Gospel Contemplation Is

In the *Spiritual Exercises*, Ignatius of Loyola teaches the "composition of place" (*compositio loci*). First you build the scene in your mind: see the place, hear the voices, then walk in with all five senses (*Exercises* 47, 121-125). He also teaches the examen each night: look back over the day and ask where God was, and where I was (*Exercises* 43).

Today we compose the place, walk onto the Mount of Olives, and end with an examen at night.

## Before and After

Chapter 13 opens with the disciples admiring the temple's great stones. Jesus says not one stone will be left on another (Mark 13:1-2). A few days earlier he had overturned the money changers' tables in that very temple, quoting Jeremiah: "But you have made it a den of robbers" (Mark 11:17; Jer 7:11). The old sin had come back: doing one's own business in God's name.

Chapter 13 ends, and chapter 14 begins at once: "It was now two days before the Passover… And the chief priests and the scribes were seeking how to arrest him by stealth" (Mark 14:1).

## Composing the Place: Dusk on the Mount of Olives

Close your eyes.

You are sitting on the slope of the Mount of Olives. Under you is hard dirt and loose stone. Around you are gray-green olive trees, their trunks twisted like old hands. The sun is going down in the west. Across the valley the temple turns gold, its white stones blazing. A wind comes up from the valley with the smell of sheep and cooking smoke.

Jesus sits beside you, facing the temple. Four disciples lower their voices and ask him: When?

## Hearing Him with Five Senses

**Hear.** He says the sun will be darkened, the moon will give no light, the stars will fall (Mark 13:24-25). It is very quiet. Only the wind. You look up for the first star.

**See.** He points to a fig tree. In winter its branches are pale gray. He says when the twigs go soft and put out leaves, you know summer is near (Mark 13:28).

**Touch.** You reach out and touch a branch. Hard. Cold. But the bud is already inside.

**Smell.** Night is coming on. In the city, lamps are being lit. You smell the oil.

**Taste.** Your mouth is a little dry. He says, "Stay awake." Suddenly you do not want to sleep.

## The Four Watches

Jesus says the master may come "in the evening, or at midnight, or when the rooster crows, or in the morning" (Mark 13:35).

Take one more step. Two days later, on the same hill.

Evening: the Last Supper, bread and cup in your hands (Mark 14:17-25). Midnight: Gethsemane, the stones cold, Jesus face down in prayer, "Abba, Father" (Mark 14:32-36). He comes back and finds the disciples asleep (Mark 14:37), just as 13:36 warned. Rooster crow: Peter by the courtyard fire, saying three times he does not know him (Mark 14:72). Morning: Jesus bound and handed to Pilate (Mark 15:1).

Through all four watches, only one man stayed awake.

## Discernment: Awake, or Keeping Watch

Ignatius teaches us to tell consolation from desolation (*Exercises* 313-336). Today, tell apart two kinds of wakefulness.

One kind leaves you emptier the longer it lasts: scrolling past midnight, worrying until dawn, the mind more and more scattered. The other kind leaves you steadier: keeping watch by a sickbed, praying for a child, knowing in your heart the master will come home.

The first is being dragged by the powers. Mark 13:25 says "the powers in the heavens will be shaken"; the Greek *dynameis* means powers. The second is being entrusted by the Lord.

## Today's Practice: The Evening Examen

Twenty minutes. In the morning read Mark 13:24-37 and 14:32-42, and compose the place as above.

Before bed, take five minutes for the examen:
1. Give thanks: Whom, or what, did the Lord entrust to me today?
2. Review: From morning to night, where was I awake but not watching? Where did I keep what he entrusted?
3. See the Lord: At what moment today was he awake beside me?
4. Hand over: Give what I did poorly to the Lord who does not slumber.
5. Rest: Say "He keeps watch for me," and go to sleep.

## Closing Prayer

Lord Jesus,
you sat on the Mount of Olives facing the temple,
and you knew how the night two days later would go.
That night your friends all slept.
Only you stayed awake, praying for them.
I am often awake today, and not watching.
Teach me to discern
which wakefulness hollows me out,
and which is the watch you entrust to me.
Tonight I give myself to you.
You do not slumber. You keep watch for me. Amen.

# Toward Advent 1 · Friday · Confirmatio, Following Christ

**November 27, 2026**
Readings: Isaiah 64:1-9 / Psalm 80:1-7, 17-19 / 1 Corinthians 1:3-9 / Mark 13:24-37
Today's focus: 1 Corinthians 1:3-9 (with 1:1-2, 10-17)

## What Confirmatio Is

Paul says, "Be imitators of me, as I am of Christ" (1 Cor 11:1). To follow Christ is not to copy a man from long ago. It is to live his likeness in the Spirit, in communion with the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit. The road has two battles: one against the desires of the flesh, one against the pattern of this age (Gal 5:16-17; Rom 12:2). Today we learn to follow from the city of Corinth.

## What Kind of City Corinth Was

Corinth sat in southern Greece between two harbors. Rome leveled it in 146 BC. Julius Caesar rebuilt it as a Roman colony in 44 BC. It filled with people starting over: freed slaves, retired soldiers, traders.

Picture it. The docks smell of tar and fish, and the dockhands shout. Up high stands the Acrocorinth, a temple on its peak. Every two years the city hosts the Isthmian Games. Runners race through the dust, and the winner is crowned with pine (1 Cor 9:25).

The city ran on patronage. Whoever spoke well, knew most, or knew the rich, climbed.

Paul lived there a year and a half (Acts 18:11). By day he sewed tents with Aquila and Priscilla (Acts 18:3). His hands were rough.

## A Letter to a Church That Compared

Around AD 53 to 55, Paul wrote from Ephesus. He had heard the Corinthian church had split into camps: "I follow Paul," "I follow Apollos," "I follow Cephas," "I follow Christ" (1 Cor 1:12). They compared spiritual gifts. Even the Spirit's gifts had become rank.

Yet in 1:2, left out of today's reading, Paul calls them "those sanctified in Christ Jesus, called to be saints." These people who loved to compare were still the church of God.

## Thanks First, Then Correction

This letter has many problems to handle. Paul begins by thanking God "because of the grace of God that was given you in Christ Jesus" (1:4).

"Grace" is the Greek *charis*, free favor. "Gift" in 1:7 is *charisma*, something given for free. The eloquence and knowledge the Corinthians compared, Paul calls gifts. A gift is not an achievement. It is the giver's kindness. So Paul thanks God, not the Corinthians.

## Who Holds You Up

"He will sustain you to the end, guiltless in the day of our Lord Jesus Christ" (1:8).

"Guiltless" does not mean the Corinthians had done no wrong. They had done plenty. It means the Lord who stood at the center of judgment stands there for them. Mark tells us he drank the cup in Gethsemane and was forsaken on the cross (Mark 14:36; 15:34).

"God is faithful, by whom you were called into the fellowship of his Son, Jesus Christ our Lord" (1:9). "Fellowship" is *koinōnia*: sharing a table. Here is the root of following Christ. God called us to sit at one table with his Son, in the Spirit, in communion with the Father.

## Two Battles

**Against the flesh.** The flesh loves to compare: gifts, ministries, whose children go to which school. To follow Christ is to set comparison down and confess that all of it is gift.

**Against this age.** This age teaches us to measure people by eloquence, degrees, and connections. Paul says God chose what is foolish, weak, and low in the world (1:27-28). To follow Christ is to see people through the cross.

## Today's Practice

Twenty minutes. Read 1 Corinthians 1:1-17, not only verses 3 to 9.

Take the third step of the week: Keep What He Entrusted. Ask: What is one gift God has given me? A pair of hands that cook. A heart that listens. A job you go to every day. This week, use it to serve one person, and tell no one.

At night, examine: Where did I compare myself with someone today? Give that moment to God and say, "This too was your gift."

## Closing Prayer

Faithful God,
you called me to sit at one table with your Son.
I often compare myself with others,
and treat your free gifts as my own achievements.
Forgive me.
Holy Spirit, help me follow Christ,
to fight the comparing in my flesh,
and the way this age sees.
You will sustain me to the end.
I keep what you entrusted to me,
because you kept me first. Amen.

# Toward Advent 1 · Saturday · Sabbatum, the Sabbath Table

**November 28, 2026**
Readings: Isaiah 64:1-9 / Psalm 80:1-7, 17-19 / 1 Corinthians 1:3-9 / Mark 13:24-37
In the synagogue this week: Torah portion *Vayishlach*, "And He Sent" (Genesis 32:4–36:43; English Bibles 32:3–36:43) / Haftarah: Obadiah (to be confirmed before publication)

## What Sabbath Meditation Is

Today is the Sabbath, the eighteenth of Kislev in the Jewish calendar. Synagogues around the world read the same Torah portion and prophets. We sit at one table. First we listen to what they read; then we say what we read. The table belongs to the Lord who made heaven and earth. He chose Israel. Israel does not own him, and neither does the church.

The Old Testament in our hands came to us from our Jewish elder brothers. For more than two thousand years they copied it, memorized it, and kept it. Every time we read Isaiah or the Psalms, we eat bread they set on the table.

## Wrestling Until Dawn

Jacob is going home. Twenty years ago he stole his brother Esau's blessing. Now Esau is coming to meet him with four hundred men (Gen 32:6). Jacob is afraid.

That night he sends his family across the ford of the Jabbok. "And Jacob was left alone. And a man wrestled with him until the breaking of the day" (Gen 32:24). The man says, "Let me go, for the day has broken." Jacob says, "I will not let you go unless you bless me" (32:26).

At dawn Jacob's hip is out of joint, and his name is changed to Israel (32:28). He says, "I have seen God face to face, and yet my life has been delivered" (32:30). "The sun rose upon him as he passed Penuel, limping because of his hip" (32:31).

Another man awake all night. Jacob kept watch at the Jabbok from dark to dawn.

## What the Synagogue Reads

The rabbis asked who wrestled Jacob. *Genesis Rabbah* says it was the guardian angel of Esau (77:3). That night Jacob wrestled twenty years of trouble between brothers.

The next day Esau "ran to meet him and embraced him and fell on his neck and kissed him, and they wept" (Gen 33:4). Jacob says, "To see your face is like seeing the face of God" (33:10).

But the brothers' story does not end tidily. This week's prophet, Obadiah, judges Esau's descendants, Edom. On the day Jerusalem fell, Edom "stood aloof," and even gloated (Obad 11-12). "As you have done, it shall be done to you" (Obad 15).

Grace and grievance tangled in one family for thousands of years. Obadiah's last line: "And the kingdom shall be the LORD's" (Obad 21).

## What the Church Reads

Reading Jacob's night, the church remembers another night. In Gethsemane Jesus also wrestled in the dark with the Father, praying the same words three times (Mark 14:39). Jacob said, "I will not let you go unless you bless me." Jesus said, "Yet not what I will, but what you will" (Mark 14:36).

Jacob limped at dawn but received a new name. Jesus was bound at dawn and handed over (Mark 15:1), and won new life for us.

## One Table

At the two ends of this table, neither side can persuade the other. For two thousand years the table has held both grace and grievance. The church once wounded Jewish people with words like "Christ-killers," and left deep scars. Some voices today remember only the grievance and forget the grace.

At this table there are two mistakes. One is contempt: calling what the synagogue reads mere shadow, and refusing to hear how Jacob's night has lived in Jewish prayer for thousands of years. The other is flattery for its own sake: being so polite that we will not say we believe the one awake in Gethsemane is the face of God that Jacob saw.

Listening means learning from them that God's people may wrestle with God and say, "I will not let you go unless you bless me." It also means saying our hope honestly: the one we wait for has come and will come again. They still wait for the Messiah's first coming. We say he has come. On this we differ, and we need not pretend otherwise.

Isaiah 64:8 says, "But now, O LORD, you are our Father." On the Days of Awe, Jewish people sing *Avinu Malkeinu*, "Our Father, Our King." People at both ends of the table can say that line together.

## Today's Practice

Twenty minutes. Read Genesis 32:22-32 and 33:1-11.

Take the fourth step of the week: He Keeps Watch for Me. Before bed, think of your own Jabbok: a relationship in your family not yet made right. Give it to God. Then pray for our Jewish brothers and sisters: that the God of Israel, in his faithfulness, will keep them, and one day open their eyes to see the King who is coming.

Tomorrow is the First Sunday of Advent. Sleep in peace. The one who never slumbers is awake for you.

## Closing Prayer

God of Israel, God of Jacob,
you wrestled with Jacob at the Jabbok until dawn
and gave him a new name.
I have my own nights of struggle,
and a brother I have not yet made peace with.
Do not let me go until you bless me.
Remember your ancient people,
from whose hands we received your word.
Keep them. Open their eyes
to see the King you have appointed.
Lord Jesus, you kept watch for me in Gethsemane.
Tonight I can sleep,
because you neither slumber nor sleep. Amen.
