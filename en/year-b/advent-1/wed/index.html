Today's focus: Psalm 80 (with the omitted verses 8-16)

What Oratio Is

Benedictines have a saying: ora et labora, pray and work. The phrase is not in the Rule itself, but it names Benedict's mind well. Seven times a day the monks laid down their tools and went to chapel to sing the psalms. Then they went back to the field, the kitchen, the scriptorium. When the bell rang, Benedict said, "let nothing be put before the work of God" (Rule 43). He also told the cellarer to treat the pots and pans "as if they were the sacred vessels of the altar" (Rule 31).

So prayer does not leave work behind. It carries work into prayer, and prayer back into work. The psalms are the book for this kind of prayer. Today we pray Psalm 80.

Before and After

Psalm 80 is "a Psalm of Asaph." Asaph was a temple singer in David's day (1 Chr 16:5). The psalm names Ephraim, Benjamin, and Manasseh (80:2), all northern tribes. Many scholars think it was written when Assyria broke the north.

Psalm 79 weeps for Jerusalem. Psalm 80 weeps for Samaria. Psalm 81 calls the people to keep the feast. The tears of south and north sit side by side in one hymnbook.

A Refrain That Grows

The psalm has a refrain, sung three times. Each time, the name of God grows:

"Restore us, O God; let your face shine, that we may be saved!" (80:3)
"Restore us, O God of hosts…" (80:7)
"Restore us, O LORD God of hosts…" (80:19)

The longer they pray, the fuller their cry. Waiting does not drive them away from God. It teaches them to call him by his whole name.

"Let your face shine" comes from the priestly blessing: "The LORD make his face to shine upon you" (Num 6:25). They are not asking for a thing. They are asking God to turn his face toward them.

The Vine That Was Cut

The reading leaves out verses 8 to 16. Read them back in.

God brought a vine out of Egypt and planted it in the land. It took deep root; its shade covered the mountains (80:8-10). Then its walls were broken down. Everyone passing by plucked its fruit. "The boar from the forest ravages it" (80:12-13). "They have burned it with fire; they have cut it down" (80:16).

The vine is Israel. Isaiah 5 sings of the same vine: God planted a vineyard and looked for grapes, and it yielded wild grapes (Isa 5:1-7). This year in Mark we will hear Jesus tell a parable about a vineyard in chapter 12. Right after it comes this week's chapter 13.

The Man at Your Right Hand

In verse 17 the psalm prays: "But let your hand be on the man of your right hand, the son of man whom you have made strong for yourself!" The Hebrew is ben-ʾādām, "son of man."

The psalmist asks God to strengthen one man, so the vineyard may live again. The church hears Mark 13:26: "They will see the Son of Man coming in clouds."

Take the Refrain to Work

This Wednesday, carry the refrain of Psalm 80 into your work.

When the bell rang, the monks in the field laid down their hoes and went to sing. We have no bell, but we have other signals: the computer starting up, the red light at the corner, the kettle coming to a boil, helping a parent out of bed. Each time, take one breath and say: "O God, let your face shine."

This is pray and work: prayer passing through work like thread through cloth.

Today's Practice

Twenty minutes. Read Psalm 80 aloud, all of it, verses 8 to 16 included. Then pray the refrain three times: first for yourself, then for your family, then for the church. Each time, call God by a fuller name.

During the day, choose three things you will surely do: starting the computer, washing dishes, getting in the car. Let them be your bells. Each time, say: "Let your face shine, that we may be saved."

Closing Prayer

Shepherd of Israel,
you who are enthroned upon the cherubim, shine forth.
The walls of your vine are broken down.
My home and my church have places that have been trampled too.
O God, O God of hosts, O LORD God of hosts,
restore us.
Today, at my work,
I will call on your name again and again.
Let your face shine
on my desk and on my hands,
and we shall be saved. Amen.
