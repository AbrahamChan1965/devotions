Today: read all four, and find the line that stops you

What Lectio Is

In chapter 48 of his Rule, Benedict of Nursia set fixed hours each day for monks to read Scripture. This slow reading came to be called lectio divina. It does not hurry. It lets the word come in first. Today, read the four passages straight through, twice. The first time, watch the pictures. The second time, listen for the line that stops you.

Read aloud. The ancients always did. When Augustine saw Ambrose of Milan reading in silence, he found it so strange that he wrote it down (Confessions VI.3). Your voice slows you down, and your heart slows with it.

Four Clocks, and a Fifth

This week's readings stand at four moments.

Psalm 80 is the oldest. It names Ephraim, Benjamin, and Manasseh (Ps 80:2). It is a prayer of the northern kingdom, likely written around 722 BC, when Assyria destroyed it.

Isaiah 64 is a lament sung by exiles home from Babylon, with the temple still a heap of burnt stone (Isa 64:11). Somewhere between 538 and 515 BC.

Mark 13 is Tuesday of Holy Week. Jesus sits on the Mount of Olives, facing the temple (Mark 13:3). In two days he will be arrested.

First Corinthians is Paul's letter from Ephesus to Corinth, around AD 53 to 55.

The fifth clock is ours. Today is Monday of the church's first week. Last Sunday we ended a year before the throne. This Sunday we begin a year on a night watch.

Start with the Gospel: The Doorkeeper

The Gospel is the spine of the week. Mark 13 is Jesus' last long teaching. When it ends, the passion begins (Mark 14:1).

At the end he tells a very short story. A man goes on a journey. He puts his servants in charge, "each with his work, and commands the doorkeeper to stay awake" (Mark 13:34). Then three times: "Stay awake" (Mark 13:33, 35, 37).

Carry this picture through the week: a man at a door, late at night, not knowing what hour his master will come.

Echoes

All four readings stretch a line between heaven and earth.

Isaiah begs God to "rend the heavens and come down" (Isa 64:1). The psalm begs the one enthroned on the cherubim to "shine forth" (Ps 80:1). Mark says the Son of Man will come "in clouds" (Mark 13:26). Paul says we "wait for the revealing of our Lord Jesus Christ" (1 Cor 1:7). "Revealing" is the Greek apokalypsis, lifting a lid. All four ask the same thing: when will the lid between heaven and earth come off?

There is also a leaf. Isaiah says, "We all fade like a leaf" (Isa 64:6). Jesus says, "As soon as its branch becomes tender and puts out its leaves, you know that summer is near" (Mark 13:28). One dry leaf, one new leaf. Two ends of one season.

Looking Up: The God Who Tears Heaven Open

Looking up, all four readings point to a God who will not stay in heaven. Isaiah calls him "our Father" and "our potter" (Isa 64:8). The psalm calls him "Shepherd of Israel" (Ps 80:1). Paul says, "God is faithful" (1 Cor 1:9). And Mark's first chapter answers Isaiah's prayer: at Jesus' baptism "he saw the heavens being torn open and the Spirit descending on him like a dove" (Mark 1:10).

Looking Down: Wide-Awake Sleep

One darkness this week is hard to see. Isaiah's people were not innocent sufferers. They stood on ground scorched by their fathers' broken covenant (2 Kgs 24:3-4). But worse than the sin itself was this: "There is no one who calls upon your name, who rouses himself to take hold of you" (Isa 64:7). They had gone numb.

Our numbness is often wide awake. The screen glows past midnight. News pours in like a flood. We are exhausted, and we are waiting for no one. It is a sleep with open eyes.

Today's Practice

Give yourself twenty minutes. Before you leave in the morning, or after the children are asleep, read the four passages aloud, twice. Then sit quiet for five minutes and let the line that stopped you settle. Write it on paper or in your phone. Carry it to work, to the stove, to the hospital.

Before bed, take the first step of the week: Tear the Heavens, Come Down. Tell God one thing you cannot put into words, or one prayer that has gone unanswered a long time. Do not polish it.

Closing Prayer

Lord, the new year begins at night.
I read your word,
and I see people on scorched ground begging you to tear heaven open,
and a man at a door, waiting for his master.
I have a prayer I cannot say well.
Tear the heavens and come down,
into my house, my night, my heart.
Keep me awake this week,
not awake and scrolling,
but awake and waiting for you. Amen.
