Sunday Sermon

A Voice in the Wilderness

SUNDAY SERMON · Second Sunday of Advent · December 6, 2026
SERMON NAIL
Steadfast love and faithfulness meet; righteousness and peace kiss each other.
This Sunday | RCL Second Sunday of Advent · Year B (December 6, 2026; also Saturday evening, Dec 5)
Readings | Mark 1:1-8 (with Isaiah 40:1-11; 2 Peter 3:8-15a; Psalm 85:1-2, 8-13)
Key words | He Speaks to the Heart · Righteousness Kisses Peace · Turn and Make the Way · He Waits for You
Preacher | Rev. Dr. Abraham Chan, Golden Lampstand Ministry

Prologue: No One to Comfort Her

The week of the funeral, the phone never stopped. Casseroles lined the counter. The second week, half the calls. A month later the house was quiet. All she could hear was the kitchen clock. She sat at the table. The chair across from her was empty.

The Bible knows that silence. In 587 BC the Babylonian army broke through the walls of Jerusalem, burned the temple, and marched the people away. Someone sat in the ashes and wrote a song of grief. One line comes back five times: "She has none to comfort her" (Lam 1:2, 9, 16, 17, 21).

This is Advent, the four Sundays when the church waits for the coming of the Lord. Today's four readings answer one question: When no one comforts you, where is God?

Act One: He Speaks to the Heart

First we have to ask: why was there no one to comfort her?

Lamentations says it plainly: "Among all her lovers she has none to comfort her; all her friends have dealt treacherously with her; they have become her enemies" (Lam 1:2). Her lovers. The nations she had chased behind God's back.

The prophets used hard words. Israel was a wife who left her husband to court the great powers. Listen to one king. Hezekiah opened his treasure house and showed every room to the envoys from Babylon. When Isaiah told him his own sons would be carried off to that very city, he said, "There will be peace and security in my days" (Isa 39:8). He traded his children's future for a quiet life of his own. A century later, the Babylon she had flirted with burned her city to the ground.

That is what the Bible calls sin. The Hebrew word, like the Greek, pictures an archer who misses the mark. Israel drifted from the steadfast love of the covenant God, his chesed, the love that keeps its promises. And an arrow that misses the target hits someone else. The fathers broke faith; the children grew up by the rivers of Babylon. They hung their harps on the willows and wept when they remembered home (Ps 137:1-2).

For nearly fifty years they said to themselves, "My way is hidden from the LORD" (Isa 40:27).

Then God spoke. "Comfort, comfort my people, says your God. Speak tenderly to Jerusalem" (Isa 40:1-2). "Speak tenderly" is, word for word, "speak to her heart." This is not a government notice. This is a husband who was betrayed, bending low to a wounded wife who cannot lift her eyes.

"Her warfare is ended, her iniquity is pardoned." The debt is paid. That is our first key word: He speaks to the heart.

Act Two: What Shall I Cry?

But the messenger was tired too.

"A voice says, 'Cry!' And I said, 'What shall I cry? All flesh is grass'" (Isa 40:6). The oldest Isaiah scroll, found by the Dead Sea, reads "I said." This is the prophet's own sigh. He has watched human faithfulness bloom like a wildflower in the morning and wilt by evening.

God does not answer, "It's not that bad." He says, "The grass withers, the flower fades, but the word of our God will stand forever" (Isa 40:8). And then he comes himself: "He will tend his flock like a shepherd; he will gather the lambs in his arms; he will carry them in his bosom, and gently lead those that are with young" (Isa 40:11). He sets his pace by the slowest, most worn-out sheep.

Psalm 85 was sung after the exiles came home. The land had come back. Their hearts had not. They still prayed, "Restore us again, O God of our salvation" (Ps 85:4). Yet the psalmist sees a picture: "Steadfast love and faithfulness meet; righteousness and peace kiss each other" (Ps 85:10).

That is our second key word: Righteousness kisses peace. God does not give up justice to buy peace. He does not give up peace to keep justice. In him the two embrace. How that can happen, we will see before we are done.

Act Three: A Voice in the Wilderness

Our sermon text is the first line of Mark: "The beginning of the gospel of Jesus Christ, the Son of God" (Mark 1:1).

"Gospel" was an empire word. In 9 BC, cities across Asia Minor carved into stone that the birthday of Caesar Augustus was "the beginning of the good news" for the world. Mark takes the same word and says: the true king has come.

What was outside Mark's window when he wrote? In Rome, Nero had Christians dressed in animal skins and torn by dogs, or set alight as torches in his gardens (Tacitus, Annals 15.44). In Judea, the temple was under siege, or already in flames. The gospel did not begin in peacetime. It began in the ruins.

Then a voice rings out: "The voice of one crying in the wilderness: 'Prepare the way of the Lord, make his paths straight'" (Mark 1:3). These are Isaiah's words (Isa 40:3). John wears camel's hair and a leather belt and eats locusts and wild honey. He looks like Elijah, the old prophet of fire.

The strangest thing is the direction of travel. "All the country of Judea and all Jerusalem were going out to him and were being baptized by him in the river Jordan, confessing their sins" (Mark 1:5). People left the city with the temple in it. They walked out into the dust, waded into a muddy river, and said their sins out loud.

"Repent" means to turn around. That is our third key word: Turn and make the way. Making the way is what Isaiah described: every valley lifted up, every mountain made low, the uneven ground made level. The despairing are raised. The proud come down. The crooked become honest. The rough become gentle.

John says, "After me comes he who is mightier than I, the strap of whose sandals I am not worthy to stoop down and untie. I have baptized you with water, but he will baptize you with the Holy Spirit" (Mark 1:7-8). Untying sandals was a slave's job. John says: I am not fit to be his slave.

Act Four: The Voice They Silenced

What became of that voice?

John told King Herod, "It is not lawful for you to have your brother's wife" (Mark 6:18). He was thrown into prison. At Herod's birthday banquet a girl danced, the king swore an oath in front of his guests, and John's head was carried in on a platter. His disciples came and took his body and laid it in a tomb (Mark 6:27-29).

The voice in the wilderness was cut off at a rich man's party.

Mark says John was "arrested" (Mark 1:14). The Greek word means "handed over." Later Mark uses the same word for Jesus: "The Son of Man is going to be delivered into the hands of men" (Mark 9:31). The road the forerunner walked, the Lord would walk too. The one who comforts us walked into betrayal and death himself.

Augustine put it this way: John was the voice; Christ is the Word (Sermon 293). A voice sounds and is gone. The Word remains. Our reading stops with John's words. Read two verses more. Jesus comes to be baptized, and "when he came up out of the water, immediately he saw the heavens being torn open and the Spirit descending on him like a dove" (Mark 1:10). After the voice, heaven opened.

Act Five: He Waits for You

But two thousand years have passed, and he has not come back. People mock: "Where is the promise of his coming? For ever since the fathers fell asleep, all things are continuing as they were" (2 Pet 3:4). Fell asleep means died. Our parents waited all their lives and died without seeing it.

So Peter corrects the way we see time: "With the Lord one day is as a thousand years, and a thousand years as one day" (2 Pet 3:8).

A thousand years as one day: what feels endless to us is, to him, like yesterday. He is not late.

One day as a thousand years: every tick of the clock in that empty kitchen weighs heavy with him. A single turning of your heart today carries the weight of a thousand years in his eyes.

"The Lord is not slow to fulfill his promise as some count slowness, but is patient toward you, not wishing that any should perish, but that all should reach repentance" (2 Pet 3:9). "Patient" is the word for long-suffering, for holding back anger a very long time.

But God is not a soft old man who looks the other way. Paul says God passed over former sins in his forbearance because he put forward Jesus as a sacrifice of atonement, "so that he might be just and the justifier of the one who has faith in Jesus" (Rom 3:25-26).

That answers the question of the psalm. How can righteousness kiss peace? "Upon him was the chastisement that brought us peace" (Isa 53:5). The word for peace there is the same word as in Psalm 85: shalom. The punishment fell on the Son, and the peace came to us. Reconciliation is not God waving a hand over the debt. It is the Son paying with his own blood. Every extra day God waits is held up by the cross.

That is our fourth key word: He waits for you.

His patience has an end. The day will come like fire, and everything will be laid bare (2 Pet 3:10). So this week, prepare his way on the road you walk every day. Go to the one you have never apologized to, and say, "I'm sorry."

Epilogue: Meeting in the Wilderness

When no one comforts you, God has not left the room. But his comfort is not cheap. After the voice was silenced, the Son was handed over; and on the cross, righteousness and peace kissed.

Take the four key words home:

He speaks to the heart: her lovers are gone, and he has come.

Righteousness kisses peace: the Son paid the price.

Turn and make the way: turn around at the river.

He waits for you: a thousand years as a day, a day as a thousand years.

"Steadfast love and faithfulness meet; righteousness and peace kiss each other." May that meeting happen in your wilderness. Amen.
