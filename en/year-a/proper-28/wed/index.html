What Oratio Is

Guigo II said that when the reader can chew no longer, he begins to ask. Prayer is reading turned into a cry. Today we do not explain. We pray. We use a temple singer's prayer, Psalm 123, and the cry of Judges 4. The New Testament can wait until Friday.

A Song for the Road

Psalms 120 to 134 are the Songs of Ascents. Israel sang them on the climb to Jerusalem for the feasts. Psalm 123 has four verses. It is short as a breath. The psalm gives no date. Many readers hear in its last line, "the contempt of the proud," the years after the exile, when the builders of Jerusalem's walls bore their neighbors' scorn (Neh 2:19; 4:1-4). That is a guess. Whatever its year, pilgrims carried it up the hill, and the church and the synagogue still sing it. The scorned have never run short.

It begins with one voice. "To you I lift up my eyes, you who sit in the heavens." By the second verse the voice is plural. One person looks up. A crowd looks up with him.

Eyes and a Hand

"Behold, as the eyes of servants look to the hand of their master, as the eyes of a maid to the hand of her mistress; so our eyes look to the LORD, our God, until he has mercy on us" (Ps 123:2).

In an old house the servant stood by the master and watched his hand. A lift of the fingers meant water, or the door, or leave the room. In four short verses "eyes" comes four times and "hand" twice. The whole psalm turns on this. Which hand do the eyes watch?

God "sits in the heavens." The Hebrew says he keeps sitting. He has not left the throne. The crushed fear an empty sky most. The psalm says the sky is not empty.

Two Cries, Two Scorns

The second half repeats a cry. "Have mercy on us, LORD, have mercy on us" (123:3). Chonnenu. It asks for grace that does not ask what we deserve. It is the root of the name God spoke to Moses on Sinai: "merciful and gracious" (Exod 34:6). The singer asks God to be who he already is.

Then the singer repeats a wound. "We have endured much contempt. Our soul is exceedingly filled with the scoffing of those who are at ease, with the contempt of the proud" (123:3-4). We are full of scorn, the Hebrew says, like a man who can eat no more.

The first half looks to God's hand. The second half tells what human hands have done. This is the voice of Judges' twenty years. The chariots pressed down. The people did not say, "We must try harder." They cried out (Judg 4:3). The cry was not defeat. It was a turning of the eyes, from the iron to the hand.

The answer came as a word. God sent Deborah. God said, "I will deliver him into your hand" (Judg 4:7). The hand moved first in speech, then in rain.

The Corner Where Scorn Grows

Be honest here. A wound of contempt grows new contempt. We are looked down on, and we turn and look down. The ones called useless are often quickest to call others useless. This is a break between person and person. It is the dark's most hidden work. It need not make us hate God. It only makes us despise people. The "proud" of the psalm are, in Hebrew, those who have risen high. Each time we look down on someone, the heart climbs a little.

So the psalm prays for the scorned and for the scorner. Eyes on God's hand keep our own fingers from pointing.

Today's Practice

Give it twenty minutes. Read Psalm 123 three times, with a minute of silence between. First, stand among the oppressed of Judges and look up. Second, look up on behalf of someone scorned today. Third, stand in your own place, remember someone you have looked down on, and set that person in God's hand. End by saying twice, "Lord, have mercy on us."

Closing Prayer

You who sit in the heavens,
I lift my eyes to you.
You are still there.
The sky is not empty.
As a servant watches the master's hand,
I watch your hand.
Have mercy on us, Lord.
Have mercy on us.
We are full of scorn.
I have scorned others too.
Heal the wound.
Do not let it sprout new contempt.
Keep my eyes on your hand
until you have mercy on me. Amen.
