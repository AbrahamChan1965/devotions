What Meditatio Is

Meditation chews. Gerard Zerbolt of Zutphen, a fourteenth-century Dutch brother, said to question a text word by word until it begins to question you. Today we chew Judges 4.

The Plain and the Iron

Start with the map. Jabin rules from Hazor, a great city in the north. His captain, Sisera, owns nine hundred iron chariots. On the flat floor of the Jezreel Valley, nothing stands against them. Israel lives in the hills and owns no iron. For twenty years Sisera "mightily oppressed" them (Judg 4:3).

Far to the south, in the hill country of Ephraim, Deborah sits under a palm. She is a prophet and a judge. The Hebrew verbs keep her still and steady: she is judging, she is sitting. Through twenty dark years she stays in her place and hears the people out.

Again

The chapter opens with a small word. "The children of Israel again did that which was evil" (Judg 4:1). Again, vayyosifu, means "they added." The book runs in a circle. Israel turns away. God sells them. They cry out. God raises a rescuer. They turn away again.

The word is honest. It names a darkness in us. We go back to the same place. Not because we do not know better. Something in us pulls, and we follow. The book took its final shape after Israel went into exile. Men by the rivers of Babylon read, "The LORD sold them," and knew whose story it was.

Into Your Hand

God sends no army. He sends a sentence. Deborah calls Barak and asks, "Hasn't the LORD, the God of Israel, commanded?" (Judg 4:6). The order is already given. Barak has not moved.

Then two verbs, and God is the subject of both. "I will draw Sisera to you … and I will deliver him into your hand" (4:7). Barak does not earn the victory. God sets it in his palm. Barak's part is to take it and walk.

Deborah's song tells how it went. "The river Kishon swept them away" (Judg 5:21). Rain fell. The river rose. Nine hundred chariots sank in mud. The iron they trusted held them down.

But Barak answered, "If you will go with me, then I will go; but if you will not go with me, I will not go" (4:8). God had spoken. Barak still wanted something he could see. This is a quieter darkness. Not open revolt. Only a heart that will not lean on God alone.

On the day of battle Deborah says, "Go; for this is the day in which the LORD has delivered Sisera into your hand. Hasn't the LORD gone out before you?" (4:14). The God who puts the victory in your hand walks ahead of you. To take it is not to shoulder a load. It is to follow.

Five hundred years later the prophet Zephaniah carries a lamp through Jerusalem. He hunts for people who say in their hearts, "The LORD will not do good, neither will he do evil" (Zeph 1:12). Deborah's torch shows people God has spoken to who have not moved. Zephaniah's lamp shows people who think God never moves.

The Last Handing Over

In this week's gospel a master hands over his goods. Both stories show a God who places things in human hands. At the end, he places his Son there. On the night of his arrest Jesus says, "The Son of Man is betrayed into the hands of sinners" (Matt 26:45). In Judges, God gives the enemy into Israel's hand. At the cross, God gives his Son into ours.

Today's Practice

Give it twenty minutes. Read Judges 4:1-8 slowly. Mark each verb whose subject is God. Mark the word "again." Mark Barak's "If you will not go with me." Then sit quiet and ask: What dark place do I keep going back to? What has God already told me to do, while I wait for something I can see? Tell God. Then read 4:7, "I will deliver him into your hand," and let it rest.

Closing Prayer

God under the palm tree,
you spoke through twenty dark years.
I have my own "again."
I go back to the same ground.
You have already told me.
I wait for something I can see.
You say, "I will put him in your hand,"
and I count the chariots.
Send your rain.
Sink the iron I lean on.
You move. You have always moved.
In the end you gave your Son.
Help me take what you hand me,
and walk behind you. Amen.
