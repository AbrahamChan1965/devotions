What Sabbath Reflection Is

Today is the Jewish Sabbath. In synagogues around the world a portion of the Torah is read in yearly order, called a parashah, with a reading from the Prophets.

Today there is one table, the table of the Old Testament. At one end sit our Jewish elder brothers. They received these words first and have read them aloud, generation after generation. At the other end sits the church, and behind it two thousand years of memory in Europe. Both ends read the same book. Across that table they have often looked down on each other. Today we say so.

Yesterday's question was: If I am not appointed to wrath, what am I still afraid of? Today the question turns. We have received mercy in Christ. How do we look at the one across the table?

What the Synagogue Reads

Today's portion is Toldot, "generations." Isaac and Rebekah have twins, Esau and Jacob. Esau comes in from the field, faint, and trades his birthright for a bowl of red stew. "So Esau despised his birthright" (Gen 25:34). Rashi, the eleventh-century Jewish commentator, draws on older rabbinic tradition. Esau asked what service the firstborn owed. He heard of many commands and penalties. He said, "I am going to die by it. What good is it to me?"

The prophetic reading is Malachi. God's first word is, "I have loved you." The people answer, "How have you loved us?" (Mal 1:2). God goes on: "If I am a master, where is the respect due me?" He rebukes those "who despise my name" (1:6). "Despise" in Malachi and "despised" in Genesis share one Hebrew root, bazah. Malachi writes like a debate. God speaks. The people ask, "How?" God answers.

Contempt at Both Ends

These twins were later taken up by both ends of the table, each to belittle the other.

At the church's end: the Epistle of Barnabas (chapter 13), early in the second century, and Tertullian's Against the Jews (chapter 1), early in the third, read "the elder shall serve the younger" as the Jews serving the church. Late in the fourth century, John Chrysostom preached eight sermons Against the Jews in Antioch (386–387) and shamed the synagogue in hard words.

At the Jewish end: rabbinic writings used Esau and Edom as a code name for Rome. After Rome turned Christian, medieval Jewish writings often called Christendom Edom. The Toledot Yeshu, passed along from late antiquity into the Middle Ages, cast Jesus as a bastard and a sorcerer. Its title borrows the very word that names today's portion.

These sources can be read. This is not one side's tale. Psalm 123 says, "Our soul is exceedingly filled with … the contempt of the proud." For two thousand years both ends have prayed it. Both ends have made the other pray it. The contempt is not only in the records. It hides in a corner of my own heart.

What We Can Do

In the twenty-first century we cannot change our Jewish brothers. To open eyes has always been God's own work. What we can do is first confess the contempt at our end. Then pray for them. Ask God to open their eyes, that one day they may see the King who is coming again, Jesus Christ, the Messiah they have looked for these thousands of years. Further on, Malachi says, "The Lord, whom you seek, will suddenly come to his temple" (Mal 3:1). We wait for him with them.

Today's Practice

Give it twenty minutes. Read Genesis 25:29-34 and Malachi 1:2, 6 slowly, then Psalm 123. Be still, and ask plainly: Do I look down on Jewish people? Tell God. Then pray for one Jewish family or one synagogue. If your town has a synagogue, say its name. Ask God to give them peace, to open their eyes, to show them the King who is coming. End with one sentence: "Lord, have mercy on us, and on them."

Closing Prayer

Lord, maker of heaven and earth,
you chose Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob.
You are the Holy One of Israel.
No one owns you.
Today the synagogue reads your word.
At this end of the table, I confess my contempt.
You say, "I have loved you."
We all ask, "How have you loved us?"
Have mercy on us, and on them.
I cannot change a single heart.
You can.
Open the eyes of my elder brothers
to see the King who is coming,
Jesus Christ, for whom we wait together. Amen.
