Saturday Dialogue: At the Oaks of Mamre

A written, imagined dialogue: we sit beside our elder brothers in the Old Testament, listen to what they read in synagogue this week, and share — from a Christian perspective — what we hear. Not debate. Shoulder to shoulder. But this week's message has an edge, and we won't blunt it.

Our elder brother speaks first. This week's Parashat is Vayera — "He appeared." The LORD appears to Abraham at Mamre's oaks, "in the heat of the day." Seeing three men, Abraham *runs* to meet them. Jewish tradition treasures hachnasat orchim (welcoming guests): Abraham set aside even his conversation with God to welcome travelers.

What we hear. Christians remember Hebrews 13:2 — "some have entertained angels unawares." And we remember this week's Gospel: Matthew 23, the Pharisees who "love the place of honor at feasts." Same feast, two postures: Abraham runs to serve; the Pharisees sit to be served. Runners are tenants; rent-collectors are landlords. Abraham had everything, yet never played the owner — in the promised land, he owned only a burial plot (Gen 23). That is the difference.

Our brother continues. Sarah overhears "about this time next year... you shall have a son" and laughs. God asks: "Is anything too hard for the LORD?" The Haftarah (2 Kings 4) echoes: the Shunammite welcomes Elisha, receives a son; the son dies; Elisha raises him. Impossible births are God's signature.

There's a detail in Vayera Christians often rush past: Abraham intercedes for Sodom. "Shall not the Judge of all the earth do what is just?" (Gen 18:25). The tenant argues with the Owner — on behalf of the guilty city. That is what tenants do at their best: they don't manage the estate, they plead for it. Contrast the third generation, who stopped pleading and started managing. Intercession is the opposite of ownership: the intercessor says "spare them"; the owner says "they owe me." Abraham at his best is the anti-landlord — and centuries later, the Son he foreshadowed would intercede from a cross: "Father, forgive them."

What we hear. "Is anything too hard for the LORD?" — that's Joshua 3's question. Flooded Jordan, impassable; worn-out Sarah, barren. The same God stops water and gives laughter. But here is the edge, spoken honestly: God gave Sarah a son, the Shunammite a son, Israel dry ground — grace again and again, and people again and again turned it into property. From Isaac to Matthew 23, the disease never changed: dwelling long in the land of grace, thinking oneself the owner. Our brother's text records it all without cover-up; we who read dare not cover it either.

Our brother reaches the heaviest passage. Genesis 22, the Akedah (the Binding): God asks Abraham for Isaac. Isaac asks, "Where is the lamb for a burnt offering?" Abraham answers, "God will provide for himself the lamb" (Gen 22:7–8). In the end, a ram instead of Isaac — Isaac lives.

What we hear. Here the edge must not be blunted. Isaac lived — but the estate-owner's Son did not. He too was led up a hill, carrying wood (John 19:17), asking why — and no ram came, because He Himself was the Lamb. Abraham heard "God will provide," and God truly did provide for Himself: not something instead of Isaac, but Someone instead of the world. The tenants killed the estate-owner's Son — yet the Son's death became the ransom for the tenants' very sin. Brother, thank you for reading the Akedah generation after generation until we could pick up the thread: you saw a ram on the mountain; we see the Lamb on the cross — the same providing God.

One more edge, and we say it with love: the Akedah ends with Isaac alive because a ram was provided. But every honest reader of Genesis 22 feels the question Isaac's story leaves hanging — what if there had been no ram? The Christian answer is that there was a day with no ram: Good Friday. The Owner did not spare His own Son (Rom 8:32). That is what makes the tenants' ransom so staggering — the Owner paid it Himself, in the currency of His Son's blood, for the very crime of killing Him. No religion on earth talks like this.

So this Saturday the message is sharp — and tender. Sharp: we are all the third generation, tenants one step from becoming landlords. Tender: the Owner didn't repossess the vineyard; He sent His Son. And the Son didn't come with an army; He came with a cross. The Judge's tears (Matt 23:37) were already flowing in Genesis 22.

Let's pray: God of Abraham — God of our elder brothers in the Old Testament — Owner who sent His Son into the vineyard, we thank You. At Mamre's oaks You taught us to run and serve, not sit and collect. Again and again You gave us sons, dry ground, laughter. And most of all: when we finally killed Your Son, You didn't destroy the vineyard — You let His blood become the ransom for this very sin of ours. Keep us forever remembering: we are ransomed tenants, never owners. Amen.
