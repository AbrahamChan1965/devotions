Sunday Sermon

A New Master — Your Seat, Your Shoulders, Your Fingers, Your Steps

SUNDAY SERMON · November 1, 2026
SERMON NAIL
Your seat, your shoulders, your fingers, your steps — all under a new master.
This Sunday | RCL Proper 26 (November 1, 2026)
Readings | Matthew 23:1-12 (with Joshua 3:7-17; 1 Thessalonians 2:9-13; Psalm 107)
Preaching time | approx. 14 minutes

The nail for the whole sermon, driven first: your seat, your shoulders, your fingers, your steps — all under a new master.

Today I ask only one question: whom does your body obey? Not your opinions, not your positions — your body. Where you sit, what you carry, where your finger points, where your feet walk. The body does not lie. The body knows who the master is.

## 1. Four parts in the temple court

Nail: the Pharisees' bodies obeyed the old master.

First, the time. Matthew 23 takes place on the Tuesday of Holy Week — Jesus' last public teaching in the temple court (Matthew 21:23–24:1). Within days He would be killed. The Church reads it at the end of Ordinary Time because Matthew 23 through 25 runs straight toward the final judgment and Christ the King Sunday: first hear judgment, then welcome the King.

Who were the Pharisees? For those hearing Scripture for the first time: they were the religious teachers of Israel, strict in the law, widely respected. To "sit on Moses' seat" (Matthew 23:2) meant to sit in the teaching authority of the law — the seat itself was right; the problem was the sitters.

Look at their four parts.

The seat. "They love the place of honor at feasts and the best seats in the synagogues" (Matthew 23:6), "and greetings in the marketplaces and being called rabbi by others" (Matthew 23:7). Rabbi means teacher. The seat must be high; the title must ring. Look further at the dress: "They do all their deeds to be seen by others" — phylacteries widened, fringes lengthened (Matthew 23:5). Phylacteries are small boxes holding Scripture; fringes are tassels on the garment's edge, meant to remind the wearer of God's commandments. Widened and lengthened, they became reminders for others to look at the wearer.

The shoulders. "They tie up heavy burdens, hard to bear, and lay them on people's shoulders" (Matthew 23:4). Rules are made for others; loads are carried by others.

The finger. "But they themselves are not willing to move them with their finger" (Matthew 23:4). The finger has exactly one use: pointing at others.

The feet. "You travel across sea and land to make a single proselyte" (Matthew 23:15), and the result is someone "twice as much a child of hell" as yourselves. The feet travel furthest, in the direction of doubling bondage.

Carry these four parts into today without looking for ancient people. The seat: who sits at the center of the meeting, whose name is engraved on the plaque, whose title is printed in the bulletin. The shoulders: who makes the rules, who carries the loads. The finger: who gets named in the group chat, who gets screenshotted. The feet: how far have you walked for one person's faith, or have you only walked with your mouth? The clothes changed in the courtyard; the body's language did not.

Then the mirror turns. "But you" (Matthew 23:8) — the moment those two words sound, everyone in the court knows: what follows is not about them; it is about us. Jesus turns to His disciples, and to everyone sitting here today.

One clarification first, lest the whole sermon go astray: Jesus does not reject Moses' teaching. He says, "Do and observe whatever they tell you" (Matthew 23:3). He Himself wore tassels (Matthew 9:20; 14:36). This passage is not an anti-Jewish text; it is a Jew warning His disciples: the seat can be right while the sitter is wrong.

The Pharisees' bodies obeyed the old master.

## 2. Jesus' four parts

Nail: Jesus' body obeyed the new master.

In the same court, the same four parts — Jesus does them all in reverse.

The seat. He "sat at table with tax collectors and sinners" (Matthew 9:10). Tax collectors worked for Rome; sinners were those the religious circle had written off. The Pharisees' seat sat upward; Jesus' seat sat toward the middle, toward the bottom. And then? "God has highly exalted him" (Philippians 2:9) — He did not exalt Himself; God exalted Him.

The shoulders. "He took our illnesses and bore our diseases" (Matthew 8:17). The Pharisees laid burdens on others; Jesus laid the burden on Himself. That is why He can say, "My yoke is easy, and my burden is light" (Matthew 11:30) — set against the burdens of 23:4: one burden crushes, one burden saves, and the difference is who carries it.

The finger. His finger was not for pointing but for touching. He "stretched out his hand and touched" the leper (Matthew 8:3) — in those days, lepers were untouchable. He "touched their eyes" (Matthew 20:34), and the blind saw. The finger went from pointing to touching, from accusing to healing.

The feet. He "went down to the Jordan" (Matthew 3:13) to be baptized, and later "set his face to go to Jerusalem" (Matthew 16:21) — knowing the cross waited there, His feet went there anyway.

So He can say, "You have one instructor, the Christ" (Matthew 23:10). Instructor means teacher, master. Notice "one" sounds three times in this chapter: one Father (Matthew 23:9), one instructor (Matthew 23:10), and "you are all brothers" (Matthew 23:8). The high seat has one occupant, and it is not us; on the ground, everyone is a sibling. Seat, shoulders, finger, feet — four parts, one master.

Finally, verse 12: "Whoever exalts himself will be humbled, and whoever humbles himself will be exalted." Note the divine passive — God is the subject of the humbling and the exalting. You do not lift yourself up; God lifts you. You do not bring yourself down; God brings you down. Whose you are decides whether you rise or fall.

Jesus' body obeyed the new master.

Your seat, your shoulders, your fingers, your steps — all under a new master.

## 3. The priests in the middle of the Jordan

Nail: the road to exaltation runs through river water.

From the Gospel we return to the Old Testament type — the type being the shadow sketched in advance, fulfilled only when Christ comes.

Joshua 3. God says to Joshua, "Today I will begin to exalt you in the sight of all Israel" (Joshua 3:7). The Hebrew is ʾāḥēl gaddelkā — "I will begin to make you great." But God's way of making him great was to send him down into the water.

Watch the formation. The ark goes first — the ark is the throne of God's presence, carried on the priests' shoulders ahead of all the people. The priests' feet touch the water, and the waters "stood rising up in a heap" as far back as Adam (Joshua 3:15–16). The priests stand firm on dry ground in the middle of the river (Joshua 3:17) until all the people have crossed, and only then come up. Shoulders carrying the Presence, feet standing in the river — these are Joshua's two parts of the body. And the date: they crossed on the tenth day of the first month (Joshua 4:19), the very day for choosing the Passover lamb (Exodus 12:3) — crossing day is lamb-choosing day.

Joshua and Jesus are one name. The Septuagint (the Greek Old Testament) writes Joshua as Ἰησοῦς, the name Hebrews 4:8 uses. The first Joshua led the people across the Jordan; the second Jesus was baptized in that same Jordan (Matthew 3:13–16) and finally stood in the middle of the river of death. Philippians 2:8–9 — humiliation, then exaltation to the highest — is Matthew 23:12 fulfilled: the One who went down into the water is lifted to the highest.

God's way of exalting a person was never to seat him on a high chair; it is to get his feet wet first, to stand him in the middle of the river. And one thing more: the ark goes first. God's presence walks into the flood ahead of us; you never cross alone — the One who commands the river is already ankle-deep ahead of you. The road to exaltation runs through river water.

## 4. The true Joshua, and a Pharisee under a new master

Nail: he was still a Pharisee — only under a new master.

The type of Joshua came alive in one man. His name was Paul — formerly Saul.

Paul before: all four parts in Pharisee form. The seat: educated at Gamaliel's feet, a Pharisee among Pharisees. The shoulders: he "bound" men and women and dragged them to prison (Acts 22:4) — "bound" is the same Greek verb, δεσμεύω, as the Pharisees "tying up" burdens in Matthew 23:4. The finger: when Stephen was stoned, he stood by "approving" (Acts 7:58–8:1). The feet: he carried letters to Damascus to arrest believers.

Then, on the Damascus road, the risen Lord met him. The man who could see went blind and had to be "led by the hand" into the city (Acts 9:8) — the pointing hand, for the first time, being led.

Paul after: all four parts changed. The seat: he could have demanded respect, yet was "gentle among you" (1 Thessalonians 2:6 in the Chinese Union Version; some ancient copies read "infants"). The shoulders: he "worked night and day, that we might not be a burden to any of you" (1 Thessalonians 2:9). The finger: "these hands ministered to my necessities and to those who were with me" (Acts 20:34) — pointing hands became working hands. The feet: from the Damascus road to the gospel road, urging people to "walk in a manner worthy of God" (1 Thessalonians 2:12) — "walk" is περιπατεῖν, walking, going on foot.

Someone will ask: Matthew 23:9 says "call no man your father on earth," yet 1 Thessalonians 2:11 has Paul "like a father" to them — is this a contradiction? The answer is in the same chapter: in 1 Thessalonians 2 Paul uses four family images — infant, nurse, father, orphan (2:17, ἀπορφανισθέντες, "orphaned"). Father here is a role, not a title: the nurse feeds, the father exhorts, the infant is tender, the orphan longs — all of them describe self-giving service, not a seat above others.

And the most moving line of all: years later, standing before the council, Paul still says, "I am a Pharisee" (Acts 23:6). His identity did not change; whom he obeyed did. He was still a Pharisee — only under a new master.

He was still a Pharisee — only under a new master.

Your seat, your shoulders, your fingers, your steps — all under a new master.

## 5. How is this possible?

Nail: not by trying harder, but by a new master.

Here someone must ask: how is this possible? How does a person change from a Pharisee's body to Jesus' body? The answer cannot be "try harder" — sitting up straighter, carrying more, being nicer: that is still the old master's road. The answer must begin with a declarative sentence — God has already done it — and only then a command: so you can.

The declarative sentence is this: the same Spirit who was first upon Christ is now within us. At the Jordan, when Jesus was baptized, "the Spirit of God descended like a dove and alighted on him" (Matthew 3:16). The prophet had said long before: "I will put my Spirit upon him... a bruised reed he will not break" (Matthew 12:18–20). Calvin called the Spirit the bond that joins Christ to us (Institutes III.1.1) — once the bond is tied, Christ's body becomes our body.

Each of the four parts has its ground. The seat: we have received "the Spirit of adoption" (Romans 8:15); God "raised us up with him and seated us with him in the heavenly places" (Ephesians 2:6) — those who wanted to be called (Matthew 23:7, καλεῖσθαι) have become those whom God is calling (1 Thessalonians 2:12, τοῦ καλοῦντος). A title is given by people; a calling is given by God. The shoulders: the gentleness of the Spirit-anointed One (Matthew 12:18–20; 11:29), so that we "bear one another's burdens" (Galatians 6:1–2) — gentleness is not weakness; only the Spirit-filled can carry weight. The finger: the law was "written with the finger of God" (Exodus 31:18), and "the finger of God" is the Spirit (Luke 11:20, compare Matthew 12:28) — now written "not on tablets of stone but on tablets of human hearts" (2 Corinthians 3:3). The feet: "I will put my Spirit within you, and cause you to walk in my statutes" (Ezekiel 36:27) — "walk" is the literal sense; "walk by the Spirit" (Galatians 5:16, 25).

Hear one more prophetic word, for those who think "I cannot": "a bruised reed he will not break, and a smoldering wick he will not quench" (Matthew 12:20). The Spirit's work is not to repair you first and use you later; it is to dwell in you first, and straighten you slowly. Once the bond is tied, you are part of His body — not because you stand straight, but because you are held.

And one sentence to nail down: Christ offered Himself "once for all" (Hebrews 10:14) — ephapax. Your standing is not a repetition of His offering; it is a sharing in what He has already finished. You need not bleed again; you stand in the river where blood has already flowed.

So the command can stand: you can stand up, you can lay down, you can touch, you can walk. Not because you try harder, but because the master has changed, and the body has a new obedience.

Not by trying harder, but by a new master.

## 6. The seated one stood up

Nail: the seated one stood up.

Finally, one picture. Genesis 18:1–8: in the heat of the day, Abraham sat at the door of his tent. Seeing three men, he ran to meet them, bowed to the ground, asked them to wash their feet (Genesis 18:4), hurried into the tent to prepare bread, ran to the herd for a tender calf, and at last stood beside them while they ate. The seated one stood up; the standing one ran; the running one served a meal. Yesterday, the synagogue happened to read this very passage — I describe the fact and nothing more.

We live in a sitting age. The phone has given us the longest finger of all — without standing up, we can judge the whole world; without leaving the house, we can condemn a person; without laying anything down, we can sit on Moses' seat. Never has sitting been so easy; never has standing been so hard.

The gospel calls us to stand up — but not by propping ourselves up. The strength to stand comes from the declarative sentence of section five: the Spirit already dwells in you; your body already has a new master.

This week, do one thing: stand up from one seat. One seat where you have sat judging — in the group chat, at the dinner table, in the meeting — stand up and go serve that person. One seat where you have sat waiting to be served — at home, in church, waiting for someone else to bend first — stand up and go welcome that person. Not all four seats; one is enough. And when you stand, say this:

"Holy Spirit, today this body is yours."

The seated one stood up.

Your seat, your shoulders, your fingers, your steps — all under a new master.

## 7. Closing prayer

Nail: your seat, your shoulders, your fingers, your steps — all under a new master.

Looking back, to gather the net. The four parts we have walked:

| Hook | Text | From… to… | |---|---|---| | Seat | Matt 23:2, 6–7; Josh 3:17 | From being called by people, to being called by God; from the high seat, to the middle | | Shoulders | Matt 23:4; Josh 3:14–15 | From laying burdens on others, to carrying them yourself | | Fingers | Matt 23:4; Luke 11:20 | From pointing fingers, to touching hands and working hands | | Steps | Matt 23:15; Josh 3:15–16 | From the Pharisees' road, to the gospel road |

Last, we end with Psalm 107. "Let the redeemed of the LORD say so." (Psalm 107:2) The redeemed — the bought-back. He "led them by a straight way" (Psalm 107:7), "and there he lets the hungry dwell, and they establish a city to live in" (Psalm 107:36). The redeemed no longer sit on high collecting rent; they are led onto a straight road and settled in a city fit to live in.

Let us pray, handing the four parts back to God, one by one.

Lord of the seat, you seat us with Christ in heaven, yet never seat us above our brothers. Take back our love of the high seat.

Lord of the shoulders, you bore our weakness and carried our disease. Take back our hands that lay burdens on others.

Lord of the fingers, you wrote on stone with your finger and now write on hearts. Take back our pointing fingers, and give us touching hands and working hands.

Lord of the feet, you went down to the Jordan and set your face toward Jerusalem. Take back our feet that roam sea and land, and give us feet for the gospel road.

Holy Spirit, today this body is yours. Make the seated among us stand up.
