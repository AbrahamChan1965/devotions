Generation After Generation Seizing God's Deed

Time Coordinate. The Matthew 23 passage we read today was spoken on the Tuesday of Holy Week — Jesus' last public teaching in the temple court, days before He would be killed. The Church reads it at the end of Ordinary Time, and the times do not coincide; the reason is this: Matthew 23 through 25 runs straight toward the final judgment and Christ the King Sunday, and looking back at judgment at year's end is how we prepare for the King of Advent.

This week's texts are fierce. In Matthew 23 Jesus pronounces seven "woes" on the scribes and Pharisees — the most respected, most Bible-literate people in the society. To read it rightly, you need generations-long eyes.

Generation one died in the wilderness. The exodus generation — all but Joshua and Caleb — fell in the desert. They saw the Red Sea parted yet wouldn't trust God for Canaan.

Generation two crossed the river. Joshua — whose name is Jesus (Yeshua, "the LORD saves") — led the new generation to put their feet in the flooded Jordan, and the waters stopped. Generations after the patriarchs' promise, God took the vineyard back and handed it over.

Then came generation after generation seizing God's deed. Dwelling long in the land of grace, every generation tried to make God's vineyard its own. In the judges' era, "everyone did what was right in his own eyes" (Judg 21:25). In the kings' era, Ahab seized Naboth's vineyard — Naboth said, "The LORD forbid that I should give you the inheritance of my fathers," so Jezebel had him killed on false charges and Ahab took the vineyard (1 Kgs 21). By the New Testament the tenants' logic is a parable: "This is the heir. Come, let us kill him and have his inheritance" (Matt 21:38). Different methods each generation; one heart: turning God's grace into my deed.

Naboth's story deserves a slower look, because it shows the machinery. Ahab doesn't just grab; he legalizes. Jezebel writes letters in Ahab's name, seals them with his seal, and stages a trial with two false witnesses: "Naboth cursed God and the king." The seizure wears the uniform of law. That is always how God's deed gets seized in His land — never by honest theft, always by corrupted procedure: the trial, the council, the vote. By Jesus' day the machinery is religious: the seat of Moses, the widened phylacteries, the long fringes — all legitimate forms, all seized for illegitimate ownership. The forms don't change; the hands inside them do.

That is the third commandment's true meaning: "You shall not take the name of the LORD your God in vain." Taking it in vain was never about profanity — it's sinning against God in God's name: His name on the lips, His property in the hands. From Naboth's blood to the prophets' blood to John's blood to the estate-owner's Son's blood — one bloodline runs through generation after generation.

So in Matthew 23 Jesus indicts not outsiders but vineyard insiders: "They preach, but do not practice. They do all their deeds to be seen by others" (Matt 23:3, 5). Phylacteries widened, fringes lengthened, loving best seats, greetings, titles. Once the deed is seized, religion becomes facade; facade needs an audience — and God disappears, leaving performance.

Remember the date: Holy Week. Jesus speaks these words days before the Last Supper; days later He will be killed by these tenants. He knows. He speaks anyway. And that killing is grace arriving: dwelling long in the land of grace, they came to think they owned it; the tenants killed the estate-owner's Son — yet the Son's death became the ransom for the tenants' very sin.

But don't skip 23:37. After seven woes, Jesus weeps: "O Jerusalem, Jerusalem, the city that kills the prophets." The Judge's tears — as severe the rebuke, as shattered the heart.

Then the epistle. 1 Thessalonians 2: Paul "worked night and day, that we might not be a burden to any of you" (1 Thess 2:9). He had every right to live from the gospel — and laid the right down. He refused to seize God's deed. The Pharisees laid burdens on shoulders; Paul laid the burden on his own. Same week, two kinds of people: those who seize God's deed, and those who lay their rights down.

Psalm 107 is the redeemed's song: "Let the redeemed of the LORD say so" (Ps 107:2). The redeemed — the bought-back. The bought-back who seize God's deed: that's this week's Pharisee. The bought-back who remember they're bought: that's where the white-robed multitude begins.

This week, we are all tenants. Those who seize God's deed kill the Son; those who remember they're ransomed wear white.

Today's Practice (about twenty minutes). 1. Read all four of this week's lections, circle one shining line, and read it aloud. (Ten minutes.) 2. Write down three burdens on your shoulders, and beside each one write: who put this here? (Five minutes.) 3. Pray one of them aloud, handing it back to the one who put it there. (Five minutes.)

Carry-away question: Who put this on my shoulders?

Closing Prayer: O Owner of the vineyard, you sent your Son into the vineyard, and paid the tenants' ransom with Him. We confess: having dwelt long here, we reach for God's deed; your name on our lips, your property in our hands. Forgive our vain use of your name; teach us to remember that we are tenants bought back, not owners. Thank you for not taking the vineyard away, but for letting the Son's blood become our ransom. Amen.
