Joshua — Whose Name Is Jesus

TUESDAY Meditatio · 週二 默想

Catching yesterday's question. Yesterday asked: who put this on my shoulders? Joshua 3 answers: what God lays on shoulders is the ark — His presence itself. A burden that does not crush, but opens rivers.

Time Coordinate. Joshua 3 takes place forty years after the Exodus, on crossing day: the tenth day of the first month (Joshua 4:19), the very day for choosing the Passover lamb (Exodus 12:3). The Church reads the crossing at the end of Ordinary Time because a crossing is the boundary between judgment and rescue — those who walk through enter the promise; those who stay behind remain in the wilderness.

"Today I will begin to exalt you in the sight of all Israel, that they may know that, as I was with Moses, so I will be with you." (Josh 3:7)

Meditate first on the name. Joshua (Yehoshua), shortened: Jesus (Yeshua) — "the LORD saves." The one who led generation two across the Jordan to retake the vineyard was named Jesus; fifteen centuries later, the One who led the new generation across the river of death to retake the lost was also named Jesus. The first Jesus put his feet in the water and the waters stopped; the second Jesus put Himself into death and death stopped. God's way of working never changes; God's saving name never changes.

Now meditate on the generations. Generation one died in the wilderness — they saw the Red Sea parted yet wouldn't trust God for Canaan. Seeing miracles isn't trusting God; leaving Egypt isn't entering Canaan. Generation two crossed — they never saw the Red Sea, yet dared to put their feet in the Jordan. Faith isn't about how much you've seen; it's about daring to get wet. Then came generation after generation: dwelling long in the land of grace, each one, without noticing, came to think of itself as the owner. From the judges' "everyone did what was right in his own eyes," to Ahab seizing Naboth's vineyard, to Micah 3's leaders who "give judgment for a bribe and teach for a price" — the methods grew more legal with each generation; the hearts grew harder.

Set Joshua beside them. His exaltation was given: "I will begin to exalt you." No campaign, no bribes, no widened phylacteries. And God's way of exalting him was to get his feet wet before everyone — the road to exaltation runs through river water. Those who seize God's deed are the reverse: their exaltation was grabbed, at the price of rigged justice, paid-for teaching, transferred vineyards. They sat in the seats, but God wasn't among them — though their mouths said "Is not the LORD in the midst of us?" (Mic 3:11). Jesus in Matthew 23: "Whoever exalts himself will be humbled, and whoever humbles himself will be exalted" (Matt 23:12). That sentence was written for exactly these two kinds of people.

Meditate on the priests' feet: "When the feet of the priests bearing the ark were dipped in the brink of the water... the waters stood" (Josh 3:15–16). The miracle is in the feet, not the sky. God didn't part the waters and then invite; He waited for wet feet. Ask today: are you generation two, or one of the generations after? Generation two's mark is wet feet — still daring to obey, still willing to step in. The mark of those who seize God's deed is dry hands — busy with paperwork, busy with facades, feet never touching water again. Owners don't get wet; owners process transfers.

Origen saw baptism's shadow here: crossing the Jordan into the promise, as believers pass through baptism into new life (Homilies on Joshua). The river didn't change; the walkers did. Every obedience is a small crossing; every foot in the water, a small baptism. And the essence of the one who seizes God's deed is the refusal to get wet again — "The title is already in my name; why should my feet get wet?"

Consider the ark once more: it went first. God's presence walked into the flood ahead of everyone. On crossing day nobody claimed anything — they followed. Ownership language enters only after grace has been enjoyed long enough to be taken for granted. The moment Israel stopped following the ark and started processing titles, the generations-after began. The question that saves is always the trembling one — "is God with us?" — never the slogan, "God is with us," stamped on the deed.

Today, choose your Jordan. The apology you owe. The presumption of ownership you need to release. The obedience you need to begin. Don't wait for the water to drop. Remember the division of labor: stopping the water is God's job; extending the foot is yours. And remembering you're a ransomed tenant, not an owner — that is the only difference between those who cross and those who drown in their title deeds.

One more precision worth keeping: the text says God exalted Joshua "in the sight of all Israel" — publicly, verifiably. Deed-seizers also love public vindication, but theirs is manufactured: the staged trial, the bought witness, the widened phylactery. God's vindication needs no staging — just wet feet and stopped water. When your exaltation requires no props, it's His. When it requires a campaign, it's yours — and it won't survive the Jordan.

Today's Practice (about twenty minutes). 1. Read Joshua 3:7–17 slowly; when you reach "feet dipped," stop and sit silent for one minute. (Ten minutes.) 2. Write down your Jordan: one apology you owe, or one obedience to begin. (Five minutes.) 3. Pray over it, and fix the first step for today. (Five minutes.)

Carry-away question: Who stands in the water, that I may cross?

Closing Prayer: Lord whose name is Salvation, God of the first Joshua at the river, and the second Joshua, Yourself. We confess the generations-after live in us: dwelling long, we reach for God's deed; hands dry, we stop stepping in. Forgive us. Give us priests' feet, daring the flood. Keep us remembering: we are ransomed, not owners; river-crossers, not title-processors. Amen.
