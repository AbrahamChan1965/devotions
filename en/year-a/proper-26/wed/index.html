The Redeemed People's Psalm of Repentance

Catching yesterday's question. Yesterday asked: who stands in the water, that I may cross? Today the psalm answers: God Himself leads the way — "he led them by a straight way" (Psalm 107:7). The priests stood in the water; God stood behind the priests. You cross not because the priests are tall, but because God leads.

Time Coordinate. Psalm 107 opens Book V of the Psalter, most likely a post-exilic work — a people back from Babylon, learning to say "thank you" again. The Church reads it at the end of Ordinary Time: the year nearly done, the days for counting have come, and the redeemed speaking aloud is the year's fitting repentance.

"Let the redeemed of the LORD say so." (Ps 107:2)

Psalm 107 is a psalm of repentance — the redeemed people's psalm of repentance. Note the subject: the speakers aren't owners; they're the redeemed. The redeemed — the bought-back. The bought-back who seize God's deed must repent; those who remember they're bought will speak. Speaking is repentance's first act.

The psalmist counts four kinds of people: desert wanderers, prisoners in darkness, fools through sin, sailors in storms. Four dead ends, one refrain sung four times: "Then they cried to the LORD in their trouble, and he delivered them from their distress" (Ps 107:6, 13, 19, 28). The refrain itself is repentance's shape: admitting you're in trouble, crying out — no more paperwork, no more orders. Owners don't cry out; owners issue orders. Crying out is the redeemed's mark; issuing orders is the owner's. When you pray — are you crying out, or giving orders?

Read the psalm across the generations. Generation one cried in the wilderness; God gave manna. Generation two stood at the Jordan; God gave dry ground. Then generation after generation: they stopped crying — they announced, "Is not the LORD in the midst of us?" (Mic 3:11). From crying out to making announcements, from "please" to "we have" — knees straightened, mouths closed. Grace dwelt in long enough, and the redeemed became owners. For an owner to repent, there is one way: learn to cry out again.

The four groups are four mirrors held up to the generations. Wanderers finding no city (vv. 4–5): generation one, out of Egypt but never into Canaan. Prisoners in darkness, bound in irons (v. 10): every generation that traded crying out for managing. Fools through sin (v. 17): Micah's owners, wise in their own eyes. Sailors melting in the storm (v. 26): us, mid-crossing, courage dissolving. The psalmist ranks none of them — wanderer, prisoner, fool, sailor get one refrain, one rescue. Grace doesn't grade your dead-end; it answers your cry. The only ones excluded from the refrain are those who never cry — owners, who don't think they're in trouble at all.

The second half (Ps 107:33–37): "He turns rivers into a desert... and there he lets the hungry dwell, and they establish a city to live in." The same God who turns rivers into desert turns desert into city. Rivers into desert — judgment for those who seize God's deed. Desert into city — mercy for criers. Whether you are redeemed — chosen and bought back — or presuming yourself the owner of God's estate decides which side you taste. And repentance is walking from the one side to the other.

The alternate Psalm 43 is the prayer of those who haven't forgotten how to cry: "Vindicate me, O God, and defend my cause against an ungodly people" (Ps 43:1). The wronged still know how to ask; those who ask haven't forgotten who they are. The day you notice you've stopped asking — only commenting, only commanding, only counting — that's the day to pray this psalm of repentance.

There's a further precision in verse 2: "whom he has redeemed from trouble and gathered in from the lands, from the east and the west, from the north and the south." The redeemed are gathered — which means no one owns the gathering. The moment a church talks like owners of God's people — our numbers, our brand, our territory — it has crossed from Psalm 107 into Micah 3. Repentance, for a church as for a person, begins at the mouth: do we still cry out, or only announce?

Today's prayer does one thing: repent. Pick one of your troubles, kneel, confess you're redeemed not owner, and cry out. No announcements, no orders. Then say so — speak God's rescue aloud. Hidden inside the command is a promise: the moment you cry out, you discover you were never the owner — just a tenant the Owner came looking for. That's not demotion; it's homecoming. And homecoming, in this psalm, always ends in testimony — the cried-out become the say-so people, and their saying becomes someone else's courage to cry.

Notice too who the psalmist says should speak: "the redeemed of the LORD" — not the deserving, not the successful, the redeemed. The qualification for this psalm isn't a clean record; it's a bought-back one. That means the worst fool in verse 17 has as much right to the refrain as the bravest sailor. Repentance isn't polishing your résumé before God; it's admitting the deed was never yours and crying out from inside the trouble. The owners' tragedy is precisely that they disqualify themselves — not by sinning too much, but by asking too little.

Today's Practice (about twenty minutes). 1. Read Psalm 107:1–9 as the wanderer: find your own wilderness. (Five minutes.) 2. Read it a second time as the one led: circle the verbs of God's leading. (Five minutes.) 3. Read it a third time as the speaker: say one rescue of God aloud. (Five minutes.) 4. Kneel and cry out over one of your troubles. (Five minutes.)

Carry-away question: Am I willing to say aloud the way God has led me?

Closing Prayer: O God who hears crying out, you answer the refrain with rescue, and turn desert into city. We confess our knees are straightening: more owner, less redeemed; commenting much, crying little. Forgive our hearts for seizing your deed; make us people who cry out again. Make us people who say so, speaking your rescue aloud, so others hear and find courage to kneel. Amen.
