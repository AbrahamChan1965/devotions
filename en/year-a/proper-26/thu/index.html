Thursday Gospel

Seven Woes, a Cross, Tears

THURSDAY Gospel · 週四 福音默觀
This week | RCL Proper 26 (November 1, 2026)
Today | Thursday, October 29, 2026 · moving toward the Sunday of November 1

TODAY'S READINGS

GospelMatthew 23:1–12 讀經CUV ReadWEB

Catching yesterday's question. Yesterday asked: am I willing to say aloud the way God has led me? Today, do not speak yet — first listen. Walk into the temple court, hear Jesus' voice, hear it all the way to the tears, and then speak.

Time Coordinate. Matthew 23 takes place on the Tuesday of Holy Week, Jesus' last public teaching in the temple court. The Church reads it at year's end because Matthew 23 through 25 runs toward the final judgment and Christ the King Sunday: first hear the seven woes, then the judgment, then at last the King's tears.

Contemplate Matthew 23 today. Hold three words: seven woes, a cross, tears. Enter with all five senses.

See. See the temple courts. The scribes and Pharisees "sit on Moses' seat" (Matt 23:2) — the seat is right; the sitters are wrong. Jesus: "Do and observe whatever they tell you, but not the works they do. For they preach, but do not practice" (Matt 23:3). The mouth is Moses'; the hands are their own. See their dress: "They do all their deeds to be seen by others" — phylacteries widened, fringes lengthened, best seats loved, greetings loved, titles loved. This passage is the overture to seven woes — shutting heaven's door, devouring widows' houses, blind guides, whitewashed tombs, killers of prophets. Every woe is an entry in the ledger of generations seizing God's deed.

Hear. Hear this: "They tie up heavy burdens, hard to bear, and lay them on people's shoulders, but they themselves are not willing to move them with their finger" (Matt 23:4). Rules for others, exemptions for self — the false owner's move. Then hear verse 11, the chapter's only exit: "The greatest among you shall be your servant." The opposite of owner isn't tenant — it's servant. God doesn't want you as owner or as pitiful sharecropper; He wants you as servant, like Him.

Touch. Feel those phylacteries. Deuteronomy 6 inside — genuine Scripture — turned into jewelry. Touch your own: your spiritual résumé, your church title, your curated piety — which help you remember God, which have become tools for being seen? Then touch something else: within days, the hands that point in accusation will be pierced by nails. The mouth that says "woe to you" will say "Father, forgive them." Touch that contrast — that is substitution.

Smell. Smell the gunpowder. Rebuking the religious establishment to its face, in Holy Week, is courting death. He knows these people will crucify Him within days. He speaks anyway — that is walking toward the cross. Breathe in that "knowing the cost and speaking truth regardless." Then breathe in Golgotha, days later: the smell of blood. The tenants killed the estate-owner's Son; the Son's blood became the ransom for the tenants' sin. As heavy the seven woes, as heavy the cross; as heavy the cross, as heavy the grace.

Taste. Taste verse 12: "Whoever exalts himself will be humbled, and whoever humbles himself will be exalted." Bitter to the owner — medicine. Sweet to the tenant — gospel. Finally, taste a tear. The woes done, Jesus weeps: "O Jerusalem, Jerusalem, the city that kills the prophets and stones those who are sent to it... How often would I have gathered your children together as a hen gathers her brood under her wings, and you were not willing!" (Matt 23:37). The Judge's tears — He didn't come to vent; He came heartbroken. As severe the rebuke, as deep the love. The seven woes are real; the tears are real.

One more precision: the woes are spoken to people, not to a system. Jesus names no empire, no party — He names the religious leaders standing in front of Him, days before they kill Him. Judgment in Scripture is never abstract; it's always personal, always weeping, always days from the cross. That's why averaging is not kindness but evasion: it turns "you" into "people nowadays," and the sword never lands.

Come back to the text. Whatever the text says today, that is what we receive. The text gives a cross, and we preach a cross; the text gives tears, and we feel the weight of those tears. Ask yourself: is my life of faith lived to be seen, or to give life? Am I the Pharisee seizing God's deed, or the servant who remembers he was ransomed?

And hear the quietest line once more: "you are all brothers" (v. 8). After dismantling every religious title, Jesus names what remains: siblings. He doesn't abolish Moses' seat, only its abuse — "do and observe whatever they tell you." The teaching office is real; the office-holders were broken. Honoring the chair while judging the sitter: a maturity our outrage culture rarely manages.

Today's Practice (about twenty minutes). 1. Read Matthew 23:1–12 slowly; when you reach "the greatest among you shall be your servant," stop and sit silent for one minute. (Ten minutes.) 2. Choose one act of service: one person, one concrete thing, and go do it today. (Five minutes.) 3. When it is done, entrust it to God's memory alone, and tell it only to Him. (Five minutes.)

Carry-away question: Which chair do I want?

Closing Prayer: Lord walking toward the cross, who spoke truth knowing it would get you killed, who pronounced seven woes and then wept. We confess the Pharisee within: loving to be seen, loving the high seat, handing out burdens while our own fingers rest. Forgive the way our minds presume ownership; make our phylacteries smaller and our hearts larger. Teach us to be servants, not owners. Thank you for your tears, showing us the Judge's heart is broken. Amen.
