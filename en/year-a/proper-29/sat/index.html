What Sabbath Reflection Is

Today is the Sabbath, the eleventh of Kislev on the Jewish calendar. In synagogues around the world, people read the same portion of the Torah and the same prophet. We sit at one table with them. First we listen to what they read. Then we say what we read. The table belongs to the Maker of heaven and earth. He chose Israel. Israel does not own him. Neither does the church.

A Night on the Run

Jacob has stolen his brother's blessing and runs for Haran. Night falls. He "came to a certain place," puts a stone under his head, and sleeps (Gen 28:11). He dreams of a ladder. Angels climb and come down. The LORD stands above it. Jacob wakes and says, "Surely the LORD is in this place, and I did not know it" (28:16). In Hebrew, wəʾānōkî lōʾ yādāʿtî: "and I, I did not know."

That is this week's question from the other side. Before the throne the nations ask, "When did we see you?" A runaway in the dark says, He was here, and I did not know.

What the Synagogue Hears

Jewish tradition holds this night dear. The Talmud teaches that Jacob first set the evening prayer, from the words "he came to a place" (Babylonian Talmud, Berakhot 26b). The rabbis noticed that the place, hamāqôm, became a name for God, "for he is the place of the world" (Genesis Rabbah 68:9). From Jacob they learned that God is present even on the night you run.

Then Jacob kept sheep for twenty years. "By day the heat consumed me, and the cold by night, and my sleep fled from my eyes." When wild beasts tore a sheep, he did not bring it to his master. He paid for it himself (Gen 31:39-40). Today's prophet remembers it: "Israel served for a wife, and for a wife he guarded sheep" (Hos 12:12). Here is a shepherd who did the work, the very opposite of Ezekiel's kings who fed only themselves.

The haftarah ends with God's word, "I will heal their apostasy; I will love them freely" (Hos 14:4). It was spoken to Israel. Everyone at both ends of this table needs to hear it.

What the Church Hears

Jesus told Nathanael, "You will see heaven opened, and the angels of God ascending and descending on the Son of Man" (John 1:51). The church hears in it that Jacob's ladder is the Son of Man. The one on the throne this Sunday is the place where heaven and earth meet.

One Table

Two mistakes wait at this table. One is contempt: calling the synagogue's reading a mere shadow and refusing to hear how Jacob's night has lived in Jewish evening prayer for many centuries. The other is praise for praise's sake: out of courtesy, staying silent about our faith that the ladder is the Son of Man. Neither one is listening.

Listening means learning from them that God is near on the night we run, and that evening is a time to pray. It also means saying our hope plainly: the One Jacob saw is the King we wait for. The shepherd Ezekiel promised, our Jewish brothers still await. We say he has come and will come again. Here we differ, and we need not pretend we do not.

Today's Practice

Twenty minutes. Read Genesis 28:10-22. Tonight before sleep, pray an evening prayer the way Jacob did. Think back to one place you passed through today and ask: Was the LORD in this place, and did I know it? Then pray for our Jewish brothers and sisters. Ask the God of Israel, Maker of heaven and earth, to keep them in his faithfulness, and one day to open their eyes to the King who is coming again, the Messiah they have waited for these thousands of years.

Closing Prayer

God of Israel,
Maker of heaven and earth,
you chose Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob.
You stood above the ladder on the night he ran.
You stood in the places I passed today.
Like Jacob, I say it:
you were here, and I did not know.
Open my eyes.
Remember your ancient people.
Keep them, and open their eyes
to the Shepherd you have set up,
the King who is coming.
Amen.
