Sunday Sermon

When Did We See You?

SUNDAY SERMON · Christ the King · November 22, 2026
SERMON NAIL
The King hides among the least.
This Sunday | RCL Proper 29 · Christ the King (November 22, 2026)
Readings | Matthew 25:31-46 (with Ezekiel 34:11-16, 20-24; Ephesians 1:15-23; Psalm 100)
Key words | He Seeks · Hidden in the Least · Eyes Opened · We Are His
Preacher | Rev. Dr. Abraham Chan, Golden Lampstand Ministry

Prologue: One Question Before the Throne

Grace and peace to you. Our title today: "When Did We See You?"

Today the church year ends. For twelve months the church has walked with Jesus, from the manger to the cross, the empty tomb, the ascension. Now the church lifts its eyes and looks at a throne. We call this Sunday Christ the King.

Jesus painted this throne himself.

"Son of Man" was the name Jesus used most for himself. It comes from the prophet Daniel, who saw "one like a son of man" come with the clouds and receive authority, glory, and a kingdom (Dan 7:13-14). When Jesus takes this name, he says: the one who will rule the nations is me.

Shepherds in Israel often ran sheep and goats in one flock. They grazed the same hills. From far off they blended. The shepherd knew them apart.

But the surprise is not the separation. The surprise is the question.

Those on the right ask: "Lord, when did we see you hungry and feed you, or thirsty and give you drink?" (Matt 25:37) Those on the left ask: "Lord, when did we see you hungry or thirsty or a stranger or naked or sick or in prison, and did not minister to you?" (Matt 25:44)

Both sides ask the same thing. The blessed did not know they had met the King. The rejected did not know they had missed him.

So we carry this question into the Word today: When did we see you?

Act One: After He Finished These Words

Jesus spoke on the Mount of Olives, outside Jerusalem (Matt 24:3). It was his last week. Chapters 24 and 25 hold his final long teaching before the cross, and this throne scene comes last.

Then Matthew writes: "When Jesus had finished all these sayings, he said to his disciples, 'You know that after two days the Passover is coming, and the Son of Man will be delivered up to be crucified.'" (Matt 26:1-2)

Mark the order. The King who has just described his throne is bound, handed over, and nailed to a cross two days later. Matthew sets the throne and the cross two days apart.

Hear how the King judges: "For I was hungry and you gave me food, I was thirsty and you gave me drink, I was a stranger and you welcomed me, I was naked and you clothed me, I was sick and you visited me, I was in prison and you came to me." (Matt 25:35-36)

Six acts: food for the hungry, drink for the thirsty, welcome for the stranger, clothes for the naked, a visit to the sick, a visit to the prisoner.

No prayers. No fasting. No temple. Ordinary deeds, so ordinary that the doers forgot them. That is why the righteous ask, "When?"

The King answers: "Truly, I say to you, as you did it to one of the least of these my brothers, you did it to me." (Matt 25:40)

Who are "the least of these"? The church has read it two ways. Some say his disciples who suffer for his name, since Jesus promised a reward to whoever gives "even a cup of cold water" to one of these little ones (Matt 10:42). Others say all who suffer. Either way, one thing stands. The King stands with the weakest. He hides himself inside them.

Hold this key word: Hidden in the Least.

Hear one more thing. To the left the King says: "Depart from me, you cursed, into the eternal fire prepared for the devil and his angels." (Matt 25:41) To the right he says: "Come, you who are blessed by my Father, inherit the kingdom prepared for you from the foundation of the world." (Matt 25:34)

God prepared the kingdom for people. He prepared the fire for the devil. He never built that fire for us. People walk into it by missing the King their whole lives.

Act Two: God Says, I Myself Will Search

Where does this King's heart come from? Go back six centuries before Christ, about 585 BC, to the prophet Ezekiel.

Babylon had crushed the kingdom of Judah and dragged its people into exile. Ezekiel lived among the captives by the river Chebar (Ezek 1:1-3). In the twelfth year of exile a runner arrived from Jerusalem with the worst news: "The city has been struck down." (Ezek 33:21)

Then God spoke: "Ah, shepherds of Israel who have been feeding yourselves! Should not shepherds feed the sheep?" (Ezek 34:2)

The shepherds were the kings. Kings in the ancient Near East called themselves shepherds. Hammurabi of Babylon, a thousand years before Ezekiel, named himself "the shepherd" in his law code (Prologue, Code of Hammurabi, c. 1750 BC). Israel's kings ate the fat and wore the wool. The flock scattered. The nation fell.

Then God said a word that turned history: "For thus says the Lord GOD: Behold, I, I myself will search for my sheep and will seek them out." (Ezek 34:11)

The Hebrew reads hinnenî ʾanî, "Behold, I, I." God says "I" twice. The kings failed. God takes the flock back into his own hands.

"I will seek the lost, and I will bring back the strayed, and I will bind up the injured, and I will strengthen the weak." (Ezek 34:16)

Here is our first key word: He Seeks.

But Ezekiel saw darkness inside the flock too. God tells the sheep: "You push with side and shoulder, and thrust at all the weak with your horns, till you have scattered them abroad." (Ezek 34:21)

Strong sheep shove the thin ones away. No wolf did this. The flock did it to itself. Sin does its worst work close at hand, in the corners of our own hearts, in the quiet sense that this place belongs to me. So God says: "Behold, I, I myself will judge between the fat sheep and the lean sheep." (Ezek 34:20)

Then God says something strange: "And I will set up over them one shepherd, my servant David, and he shall feed them." (Ezek 34:23)

David had been dead four hundred years. So which is it? Will God shepherd them himself, or will David?

The New Testament answers. Matthew opens by naming Jesus "the son of David" (Matt 1:1). Matthew tells us that when Jesus saw the crowds, "he had compassion for them, because they were harassed and helpless, like sheep without a shepherd" (Matt 9:36).

God came himself. He came as David's son. In Jesus, "God himself" and "David's son" meet in one person. And the judgment Ezekiel foretold between sheep and sheep happens before the throne in Matthew 25.

See the order. Before the throne comes the search. The shepherd finds before he divides.

Act Three: Paul Prays for Eyes

Where is this King now? Turn to Ephesians.

Paul wrote this letter. He had hunted Christians, met the risen Jesus, and become an apostle to the nations. He wrote from prison, calling himself "a prisoner for Christ Jesus" (Eph 3:1).

Ephesus was a great city of Asia Minor, home of Artemis's famous temple. Its people feared spirits and practiced magic. Acts tells us that new believers there burned their books of sorcery in public, books worth fifty thousand pieces of silver (Acts 19:19). Paul had lived there nearly three years (Acts 20:31). When he left, the elders of Ephesus wept and embraced him (Acts 20:37).

A man in chains prays for people who live in fear. What does he ask? Not his release. Not more power. He asks for eyes:

"that the God of our Lord Jesus Christ, the Father of glory, may give you the Spirit of wisdom and of revelation in the knowledge of him, having the eyes of your hearts enlightened." (Eph 1:17-18)

Here is our third key word: Eyes Opened.

Eyes to see what? That God "raised him from the dead and seated him at his right hand in the heavenly places, far above all rule and authority and power and dominion" (Eph 1:20-21).

Do you see it? The one handed over in Matthew 26, God raised and seated on the throne. Every power the Ephesians feared lies under his feet.

Paul goes on: "And he put all things under his feet and gave him as head over all things to the church, which is his body." (Eph 1:22-23)

This brings us back to the question before the throne. Why did neither side see the King? Because no human eye can find the King in the least. The Spirit must open the eyes of the heart.

Paul's prayer is good news. We need not wait for judgment day to be surprised. We can ask God to open our eyes today.

Act Four: Why Does the King Hide in the Least?

Now the deepest question. Why does this King hide in the least?

Because he became the least first. Lay the six acts against his own life:

Hungry. He fasted forty days in the wilderness, "and he was hungry" (Matt 4:2). Thirsty. On the cross he said, "I thirst" (John 19:28). A stranger. As a child he fled to Egypt with his parents (Matt 2:13-14). As a man he said, "The Son of Man has nowhere to lay his head" (Matt 8:20). Naked. Soldiers stripped him and cast lots for his garments (Matt 27:28, 35). Sick. "He took our illnesses and bore our diseases" (Matt 8:17). In prison. They seized him, bound him, and led him to Pilate (Matt 26:57; 27:2).

The King walked all six roads. So when he says, "You did it to me," he does not speak in metaphor. He knows those places from the inside.

Above the cross hung a sign: "This is Jesus, the King of the Jews." (Matt 27:37) The throne of chapter 25 and the cross of chapter 27 belong to one King. He became the least on the cross. Then he took the throne.

This is the gospel. We could hear this passage as, "Do good, or else." But listen. The King tells the righteous to "inherit." An inheritance is a gift, not a wage. God prepared that kingdom "from the foundation of the world," long before anyone lifted a hand.

In Ezekiel's eyes, none of us comes first as the strong rescuer. We come as thin sheep, found and carried home. He sought us first.

Why did the righteous forget their deeds? They were not keeping score. The found go looking for others.

The church set this feast in 1925 (Pius XI, Quas Primas). The Great War had just ended, and nations were crowning their own power. The church answered: there is another King. His throne does not stand at the top of power. It stands among the least.

Act Five: Where Will You Meet Him This Week?

I know the life many of you live.

You leave home at seven and come back late. Or you stay home all day with small children, or you care for parents who grow weaker each year. Or you serve at church week after week until you have nothing left. If I hand you one more list of good deeds, you will nod and go home and fail, and carry more guilt than before.

The King does not speak that way. So God's Word calls us three ways today.

First, let him find you. Maybe you feel scattered and hurt, like Ezekiel's sheep. Maybe you have never opened a Bible and do not know this King. Hear him: "I myself will search." You do not have to find him. He comes for you. Answer him today: "Lord, here I am."

Second, ask for eyes. Each morning this week, pray Paul's prayer: "Lord, open the eyes of my heart." Do not ask to go somewhere else. Ask to see the King on the road you already walk: on the commute, at the table, in the coworker who always eats lunch alone. And ask him to show you yourself. At home, at work, where have I pushed with my shoulder like the fat sheep?

Third, see him where you have been placed. Look again at those on the right. They did not hunt for a list of good works. Their deeds were so plain they forgot them. That is why they asked, "When did we see you?"

So the King hides most often on the path you already walk. The supper you cook for your children after a long day. The blanket you pull over your father at midnight. The hour you sit by a sick bed. The King sees it all. He says, "As you did it to one of the least of these my brothers, you did it to me." (Matt 25:40) Your labor does not stand outside the kingdom.

What about the hungry on the street, the sick no one visits, the prisoner everyone forgets? No one person can reach them all, and no one person has to. Paul calls the church Christ's "body" (Eph 1:23). A body has many members. No member does everything. The church threads the needle.

When a family welcomes a baby or someone falls ill, the church sets up a meal rotation. Your part may be one meal a month. When the visitation team goes to the hospital, your part may be a name on a card or one evening of prayer. Some ministries serve prisoners and their families. Charles Colson founded one, Prison Fellowship, in 1976. Your part may be the moment the church prays for them.

You do not have to do it all. Do your part.

Mark the hour of this passage. Jesus spoke on the Mount of Olives to answer his disciples: "What will be the sign of your coming and of the end of the age?" (Matt 24:3) The word for "coming" is parousia. In Jesus' day it often named a king's arrival at a city (Deissmann, Light from the Ancient East, 1910). The throne scene is that King's arrival.

Next Sunday a new church year begins with Advent. Advent folds two comings into one season. The church remembers that he came, born in a manger at Bethlehem, the least of all. And the church waits for him to come again, seated on the throne of glory. We live between those two comings. In the meantime, he hides in the least.

So we do not wait by staring at the sky. We wait with open eyes in daily life. Next Sunday's Gospel says it is like a man who goes on a journey, "when he leaves home and puts his servants in charge, each with his work, and commands the doorkeeper to stay awake" (Mark 13:34). The work the Master gave you is your post: your job, your home, the people in your care. Be faithful there. Keep your eyes open there. That is how you stay awake.

Jesus said: "And what I say to you I say to all: Stay awake." (Mark 13:37)

Epilogue: We Are His

We answer the King with an old song. The Israelites sang Psalm 100 as they passed through the temple gates: "Make a joyful noise to the LORD, all the earth!" (Ps 100:1)

Its heart is verse 3: "Know that the LORD, he is God! It is he who made us, and we are his; we are his people, and the sheep of his pasture."

The Hebrew holds a small wonder here. The written text reads "and not we ourselves." The reading tradition says "and we are his." Both are true. We did not make ourselves. We belong to him.

Here is our last key word: We Are His. Only those who know they belong to this King will recognize him in the least. Only found sheep go looking for lost ones.

The psalm goes on: "Enter his gates with thanksgiving, and his courts with praise!" (Ps 100:4) In the end the King opens that gate himself. He says: "Come, you who are blessed by my Father."

Take these four key words home. He Seeks: God came looking for us first (Ezek 34:11). Hidden in the Least: the King lives among the least (Matt 25:40). Eyes Opened: the Spirit opens our eyes (Eph 1:18). We Are His: we are the sheep of his pasture (Ps 100:3).

One day all nations will stand before that throne. May we begin to see him today: at the table, by the sickbed, in a stranger's face, in the least of these.

Glory to the King who sought us, who hides among the least, who sits at the Father's right hand, our Lord Jesus Christ. Amen.

Small Group Discussion

Question 1

Read Matthew 25:37-40 first. The righteous did these things and did not remember them. The King says that what we did to one of the least, we did to him.

Question: At home, at work, at church, who is the person you most easily overlook? Looking back on this week, when might you have met the King without knowing it?

Question 2

Read Ezekiel 34:20-21 and Ephesians 1:18 first. The strong sheep push the weak aside with side and shoulder. Paul, in prison, prays that the Spirit would open the eyes of our hearts.

Question: At home, at work, at church, when are you most likely to push someone aside with your "shoulder"? This week, how will you use Paul's prayer each day to ask the Lord to open your eyes, to see yourself first and then to see others?

Question 3

Read Ezekiel 34:11 and Mark 13:34 first. God came out himself and found us first. The master gives each servant his work and tells them to stay awake. We do not strain to win the King's favor. We are found sheep, answering him where he has placed us.

Question: This week, at the post you already have (home, work, church), what one small thing can you do for one of the least? In the work the church is already threading together, what small part could you take up?
